Training decision models for simulating human respondents

Authors
Affiliation

Davood Wadi

McGill University

Yu Ma

McGill University

Abstract.
Researchers increasingly use large language models (LLMs) as synthetic respondents in market research, conjoint analysis, and randomized experiments. However, synthetic choice data from off-the-shelf generative LLMs bias downstream econometric inference. Neither proprietary models nor higher reasoning effort reliably reduces this bias. We investigate decision models (System One architectures) for silicon sampling. Using choice training, we update KEV model weights on historical respondent-level choice microdata by conditional maximum likelihood, before inference and without target-study data. Across three model scales, training on two discrete choice experiments lowers treatment-effect error by 22% to 89% on four held-out studies from new domains (vaccines, nutrition labels, consumer rights, and AI) and new response formats. A small choice-trained model outperforms a much larger off-the-shelf model on three of the four studies. Although training on single studies lowers cross-entropy, it can exacerbate treatment-effect error. Choice training thus complements post-inference calibration by providing a stronger starting point for estimating human preferences.

Keywords. Decision models, large language models, synthetic respondents, econometric inference, marketing research

Introduction

Using foundation models as synthetic respondents has attracted growing interest in marketing and behavioral research. In marketing, a primary goal is downstream econometric inference, such as estimating attribute slopes in conjoint analysis or recovering causal treatment effects in randomized experiments. Most studies on synthetic respondents generate synthetic choice data with off-the-shelf generative large language models (LLMs), yet substituting these outputs for human responses severely biases downstream analyses (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026). A likely source of this bias is how LLMs are trained. During training, LLMs are rewarded for producing a single correct answer on single-label objective benchmarks (e.g., mathematics, factual questions) and for complying with safety rules. In these tasks, any probability placed on other answers is considered error to be minimized. In contrast, simulating human choices requires a probability distribution over all plausible responses, because respondents with the same observed characteristics can differ in their preferences. Therefore, off-the-shelf LLMs tend to collapse their response distribution onto a single answer and, because of their safety training, sometimes refuse to answer. For instance, on a vaccine conjoint subsample (Kreps et al. 2020), off-the-shelf models incur large ordinary least squares (OLS) treatment-effect error (\(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\)) relative to human respondents (Wang et al. 2026). Contrary to common expectation, using proprietary models or increasing reasoning effort can exacerbate this error (Figure 1).

Moreover, generative LLMs are slow and expensive because generating text tokens requires costly Monte Carlo sampling to approximate choice probabilities. To avoid these costs, we study decision models. Decision models replace a language model’s text-generation head with a pointer head that scores candidate options directly (Deußer et al. 2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Palmer 2026). Unlike LLMs that produce a probability distributions over all tokens in the vocabulary, decision models produce a probability distributions over only the valid options in a task. These probabilities eliminate Monte Carlo sampling cost and yield exact expected OLS treatment effects. Despite this architectural advantage, off-the-shelf decision models still incur high treatment-effect error (Figure 1). Because they are trained on similar single-label objective benchmarks, they treat disagreement among respondents with the same persona as classification error (Gao et al. 2026).

In this research, we train decision models by updating their weights on historical human choice microdata. We call this procedure choice training. Specifically, we minimize respondent-level cross-entropy (CE), which is equivalent to conditional maximum likelihood estimation. Unlike post-inference calibration methods that keep model weights fixed and require human data from the target study (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), choice training occurs before inference and uses no target-study human data. We therefore ask whether choice training on a small set of historical datasets can reduce error on held-out test datasets that never enter training.

Note. Bars report OLS \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta \times 10^3\) (Equation 5) across 11 vaccine attribute levels on a 200-respondent Vaccines conjoint subsample (\(n = 1,000\) choice tasks; Wang et al. 2026). Errors are measured relative to full-sample human estimates (\(N = 1,971\) respondents; 9,855 choice tasks).

We evaluate choice training across three open-weight decision-model scales (0.8B, 4B, and 9B parameters; Palmer 2026). We train on two historical choice datasets and select the best checkpoint on a separate validation dataset. We use four held-out test datasets: a nutrition-labeling trial (Grummon et al. 2025), a vaccine conjoint (Kreps et al. 2020), a consumer-rights conjoint (Kantorowicz-Reznichenko et al. 2026), and a rating conjoint on AI and offshoring (Magistro et al. 2026). Compared to off-the-shelf decision models, we find that choice training on two studies lowers both \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) (by 22% to 89%) and \(CE\) (by 10% to 51%) on every held-out test dataset and model scale. These gains extend to unseen studies with attributes and response formats absent from training. We also find that a small choice-trained model can outperform a much larger off-the-shelf model on three of the four test datasets.

Our research makes the following contributions to synthetic respondents in marketing and behavioral research. First, we show that choice training lowers treatment-effect error on unseen studies without any target-study human data, complementing existing post-inference calibration methods (Hullman et al. 2026). Second, we show that training decision models on just two studies with binary choices transfers to new domains (vaccines, nutrition labels, consumer rights, AI, and offshoring) and new response formats (three-option choices and five-point ratings). Unlike prior research, where training on human choices failed to generalize to new domains (Brand et al. 2023) or even within the same study (Wang et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2026), our training regime transfers to unseen studies. Third, we show that training models to minimize cross-entropy loss does not guarantee improvements in treatment effect recovery. Models trained on only one of our training studies did lower the CE loss on every test dataset, yet sometimes led to worse treatment-effect error. On the other hand, training on both studies mitigated these failures. This finding motivates novel training objectives that target treatment effects directly.

Literature review

Early work on synthetic respondents prompted off-the-shelf generative LLMs with demographic profiles to simulate human responses in political, economic, and marketing surveys (Argyle et al. 2023, Horton et al. 2023, Santurkar et al. 2023, Goli and Singh 2024). Although these models can capture average preference directions, systematic discrepancies from human populations severely bias downstream econometric estimates (Santurkar et al. 2023, Wadi and Fredette 2025, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026). Common distortions include mode collapse (i.e., producing nearly the same answer every time a question is asked) and prompt sensitivity. Recent marketing research addresses these biases while keeping model weights fixed, relying on human data from the target study or target individuals. These methods include post-inference econometric debiasing (Wang et al. 2026), optimal post-inference reweighting across prompts and checkpoints (Leng et al. 2026), and individual digital twins conditioned on hundreds of responses per person (Toubia et al. 2025). Post-inference calibration uses a sample of human responses from the same study to correct model outputs. Therefore, the final estimates remain statistically valid even when the model itself is biased. However, how much these methods gain over human data alone depends on how closely the underlying model approximates the target human population (Hullman et al. 2026).

A growing literature instead updates LLM weights directly on human microdata, either from the target study itself, from a different study in the same product category, or from many unrelated studies. Training LLMs on a small sample (e.g., 50 tasks) from the same study cannot remove the bias in regression parameters (Wang et al. 2025, Ye et al. 2025, Wang et al. 2026) and still requires human data for the same study. Moreover, research has shown that WTP estimates from a separate study can transfer to a new study within the same product category, but fail to generalize to new product categories (Brand et al. 2023). Outside marketing, large-scale behavioral training improves out-of-study prediction across cognitive psychology (10 million choices across 160 experiments; Binz et al. 2025), social-science experiments (2.9 million responses across 210 studies; Kolluri et al. 2025), and public-opinion surveys (Suh et al. 2025, Ku et al. 2026, Huang et al. 2026). Yet these cross-study efforts rely on large multi-hundred-study corpora and autoregressive text generation. Moreover, they evaluate predictive log-likelihood, marginal distributions, or cell-level distributional distance rather than econometric treatment-effect recovery.

A third stream of research tackles the sampling variance of repeated text generation by using choice probabilities in a single forward pass. Early studies extract next-token probabilities over option symbols (e.g., A, B, and C) from LLMs, yet those probabilities remain sensitive to vocabulary and token-frequency priors (Kadavath et al. 2022, Wadi et al. 2026). Dedicated decision models address this sensitivity by scoring candidate options outside the vocabulary head (Palmer 2026, Wadi and Ma 2026). However, because this architecture is new, it remains open whether pre-inference choice training can adapt decision models for unseen-study econometric inference without using target-study human data. In the following section, we formalize our training and evaluation methodology for these models.

Methodology

Decision model parameterization. Consider a choice study composed of discrete-choice tasks indexed by \(t\) (Figure 2, panel a). Each task pairs observed respondent covariates \(\mathbf{s}_t\) (e.g., sex, age, education, income, political affiliation, and race), which vary by dataset, with a task context \(c_t\) specifying the instructions and \(K_t \ge 2\) discrete options. Let \(y_{t,k} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether the human respondent selects option \(k \in \{1, \dots, K_t\}\) on task \(t\), with \(\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} y_{t,k} = 1\).

Unlike LLMs that generate tokens autoregressively, a decision model replaces the vocabulary head of a pretrained language model with a pointer head that scores all \(K_t\) options in one forward pass (Figure 2, panel b). For each option \(k\), the model computes a real-valued score \(z_{t,k}(\theta)\) from \((\mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\). \(\theta\) consists of trainable low-rank adaptation (LoRA) weights (Hu et al. 2021) on the backbone and the pointer-head parameters. Applying a softmax transformation with unit temperature produces the conditional choice probabilities: \[ p_{t,k}(\theta) = P\big(y_{t,k} = 1 \mid \mathbf{s}_t, c_t; \theta\big) = \frac{\exp\!\big(z_{t,k}(\theta)\big)}{\sum_{m=1}^{K_t} \exp\!\big(z_{t,m}(\theta)\big)}. \tag{1}\] Formally, Equation 1 has the multinomial logit form, with \(z_{t,k}(\theta)\) entering in place of systematic utility. Because Equation 1 normalizes directly over \(\{1, \dots, K_t\}\) without text decoding, out-of-set outputs have zero probability by construction.

Choice training. Off-the-shelf decision models are trained on deterministic tasks with single-label answers. Therefore, the true conditional distribution of labels places all probability mass on one option. Minimizing cross-entropy on such data pushes predicted probabilities \(p_{t,k}(\theta)\) toward zero and one. In contrast, in human choice studies, respondents with identical observed covariates, \(\mathbf{s}_t\), still differ in unobserved preferences. Therefore, the true conditional choice distribution \(\mathbf{q}_t = (q_{t,1}, \dots, q_{t,K_t})\), with \(q_{t,k} = P(y_{t,k} = 1 \mid \mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\), spreads probability mass across options rather than collapsing onto a single mode.

Choice training updates \(\theta\) before inference on historical respondent-level choice microdata (Figure 2, panel c). Starting from the off-the-shelf model checkpoint, we treat each task as an independent observation given the respondent’s covariates (i.e., a pooled likelihood), although each respondent may complete several tasks. We minimize empirical cross-entropy over mini-batches \(S\) of historical tasks: \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta; S) = -\frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{t \in S} \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} y_{t,k} \log p_{t,k}(\theta). \tag{2}\] Minimizing Equation 2 is equivalent to pooled conditional maximum likelihood estimation of Equation 1. This objective is respondent-level because each term uses an individual respondent’s observed choice vector \(\mathbf{y}_t = (y_{t,1}, \dots, y_{t,K_t})\). Taking expectations over \(\mathbf{y}_t \sim \mathbf{q}_t\) conditional on \((\mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\) decomposes the expected task loss as

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}_t \sim \mathbf{q}_t}\!\left[-\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} y_{t,k} \log p_{t,k}(\theta)\right] &= -\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} q_{t,k} \log p_{t,k}(\theta) \\ &= H(\mathbf{q}_t) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\mathbf{q}_t \,\|\, \mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\big), \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

where \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) = (p_{t,1}(\theta), \dots, p_{t,K_t}(\theta))\) is the predicted choice distribution from Equation 1, \(H(\mathbf{q}_t) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} q_{t,k} \log q_{t,k}\) is the Shannon entropy of human choices, and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{q}_t \,\|\, \mathbf{p}_t(\theta)) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} q_{t,k} \log\big(q_{t,k} / p_{t,k}(\theta)\big)\) is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. Because \(H(\mathbf{q}_t)\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{q}_t \,\|\, \mathbf{p}_t(\theta)) \ge 0\) with equality if and only if \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) = \mathbf{q}_t\), cross-entropy is a strictly proper scoring rule (i.e., its expected value is minimized only by the true choice distribution).

Equation 3 resolves an apparent tension in our objective function. Choice training minimizes the same cross-entropy loss on the same one-hot label format as training regimes that have a single correct answer. What separates the two is the distribution of \(\mathbf{q}_t\). On objective tasks, \(\mathbf{q}_t\) places all probability mass on the correct option (\(H(\mathbf{q}_t) = 0\)), so minimizing the KL term pushes \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\) toward zero and one. On human choice data, if respondents with identical \((\mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\) choose differently, \(\mathbf{q}_t\) becomes interior (\(H(\mathbf{q}_t) > 0\)). Thus, the same loss function drives \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\) toward interior probabilities rather than the most common option. Each one-hot choice is a single draw from \(\mathbf{q}_t\), hence training requires no aggregate choice shares. This property is essential in randomized designs with sparse respondent covariates and task attributes, where each unique combination \((\mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\) is typically observed only once and, thus, empirical choice shares cannot be estimated cell by cell. In such cases, the model recovers \(\mathbf{q}_t\) by sharing parameters across respondents and tasks with similar covariates and attributes. Because this argument holds in expectation, a sufficiently flexible model could still memorize individual choices. Therefore, choice training should still select the best checkpoint on held-out validation data. Importantly, no target-study data enters Equation 2, so downstream econometric evaluation measures genuine generalizability to unseen studies.

Econometric evaluation. On a held-out evaluation dataset, we hold \(\theta\) fixed and evaluate treatment-effect recovery using ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions defined by the dataset’s experimental design (Figure 2, panel d). Applying the same linear specification symmetrically to both human responses and model-implied expected outcomes places the human benchmark and synthetic choice data on a common estimator. Consider each regression \(g \in \{1, \dots, G\}\) in the dataset, with full-column-rank design matrix \(\mathbf{X}^{(g)}\) and human outcome vector \(\mathbf{o}^{(g)}\). The matrix \(\mathbf{X}^{(g)}\) contains an intercept and \(d_g\) treatment or attribute indicators from the experiment design. Across our evaluation designs, each entry of the regression outcome \(\mathbf{o}^{(g)}\) is a linear function of the choice indicators \(y_{t,k}\). It is either a binary indicator \(y_{t,k}\) for choosing a given profile or target option (zero across profiles when the outside option is chosen) or the numeric rating \(\sum_{k=1}^{K_t} k\,y_{t,k}\) on an ordinal scale. By linearity of expectation, replacing each realized indicator \(y_{t,k}\) with its predicted probability \(p_{t,k}(\theta)\) from Equation 1 yields the exact expected outcome vector \(\bar{\mathbf{o}}^{(g)}(\theta)\) that would result from averaging over synthetic choices drawn from \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\).

Applying the OLS operator \(\mathbf{A}^{(g)} = (\mathbf{X}^{(g)\top}\mathbf{X}^{(g)})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{(g)\top}\) to the human and model-predicted outcome vectors defines the intercepts and slopes:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \hat{\alpha}^{(g)} \\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{A}^{(g)}\mathbf{o}^{(g)}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} \bar{\alpha}^{(g)}(\theta) \\ \bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}(\theta) \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{A}^{(g)}\bar{\mathbf{o}}^{(g)}(\theta), \tag{4}\]

where \(\hat{\alpha}^{(g)}\) and \(\bar{\alpha}^{(g)}(\theta)\) are the intercepts, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}\) is the full-sample human slope benchmark, and \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}(\theta)\) is the vector of \(d_g\) synthetic treatment or attribute slopes. Because \(\mathbf{X}^{(g)}\) is fixed and OLS is linear in the outcome vector, \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}(\theta)\) equals the exact expectation of the OLS slope estimator over synthetic choices drawn from \(p_{t,k}(\theta)\), with zero Monte Carlo variance.1

Because all \(G\) regressions within a dataset share identical outcome units, we measure aggregate slope recovery across all \(d = \sum_{g=1}^G d_g\) slopes via mean squared error (Wang et al. 2026):

\[ \mathrm{MSE}_{\beta}(\theta) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{g=1}^G \left\lVert \bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}(\theta) - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)} \right\rVert_2^2. \tag{5}\]

Alongside Equation 5, we evaluate Equation 2 across all tasks in each dataset to report held-out cross-entropy in nats (Table 2). Whereas Equation 5 measures aggregate slope recovery, cross-entropy captures how accurately \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\) predicts conditional choice distributions given \((\mathbf{s}_t, c_t)\) (Equation 3).

Experimental setup

Datasets

For choice training, we use seven datasets from distinct product and policy domains (Table 1). Two datasets provide the respondent-level training microdata: a paired electricity-plan conjoint (Electricity; Wu 2025) and a discrete-choice experiment on drone versus ground delivery across four vendor contexts (Drones; Berke et al. 2023). Both training datasets vary price (monthly bill or delivery fee) alongside non-price attributes. A separate airline conjoint (Flights; Goldfarb et al. 2026) dataset serves as our validation set, which we use to tune hyper-parameters and select the best checkpoint (lowest \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\)). Finally, we use four held-out test datasets to evaluate generalizability to new studies, domains, and choice format.

Two test datasets include prices on products with price ranges absent from training (Consumer rights and AI & offshoring), whereas the other two share no attributes with the training datasets (Food labels and Vaccines). Response formats in the test sets are also different from the binary choices used in training (Table 1). The test datasets have a three-option conjoint with an outside option (Vaccines), a three-option identification task (Food labels), and a five-point ordinal rating (AI & offshoring). Across all seven datasets, each choice task conditions on the respondent’s observed covariates \(\mathbf{s}_t\) in a standardized text format (Figure 2, panel a).

Note. Shorthand labels in the Dataset column are used throughout the paper. \(K\) is the number of response options per choice task. \(N\) is the number of respondents. \(d\) reports the total number of non-reference treatment or attribute slopes across each dataset’s experimental design (Section 3). Specifically, Consumer rights spans two product conjoints of 19 slopes each (38 total), and AI & offshoring spans four country-by-arm regressions of 8 slopes each (32 total) on a five-point support scale (1 to 5).

Choice-training regimes and protocol

At each model scale, we use a \(2 \times 2\) design crossing the inclusion of Electricity (excluded vs. included) with the inclusion of Drones (excluded vs. included) studies. This forms four choice-training regimes: Off-the-shelf (neither dataset), Electricity (18,591 tasks), Drones (36,297 tasks), and Drones + Electricity (54,888 tasks). Off-the-shelf evaluates the unmodified decision model without choice training. This design tests whether single-dataset training transfers to unseen datasets and whether pooling both choice datasets improves the model beyond each individual dataset.

We evaluate the KEV-0.8B, KEV-4B, and KEV-9B open-weight decision models (Palmer 2026), each loaded in bfloat16 precision with unit temperature. Choice training updates LoRA adapters (rank \(r = 64\), scaling \(\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}} = 128\), dropout \(0.05\)) across all linear backbone projections jointly with the trainable pointer head via Equation 2. Every trained run uses AdamW with a \(5 \times 10^{-6}\) peak learning rate, a OneCycle schedule (\(10\%\) warmup and cosine decay), weight decay \(0.01\), and gradient-norm clipping at \(1.0\), for \(3\) epochs with mini-batch size \(|S| = 40\) and random seed \(42\). Within each epoch, tasks are shuffled by dataset (and by vendor context in Drones), partitioned into single-dataset mini-batches, and interleaved randomly.

Results

Unseen-study transfer across domains and model scales

Relative to the off-the-shelf checkpoints, pooled choice training (Drones + Electricity) lowers both \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) and CE in all 12 held-out test cells, reducing \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) by 22% to 89% (Table 2). Notably, these gains extend both to datasets that share no attributes with the training studies (Food labels and Vaccines) and to response formats absent from training (Figure 3).2 In particular, although the training data contain only binary choices, pooled models still transfer to three-option choices and five-point ordinal ratings.

Training on a single dataset, however, transfers to unseen studies less reliably. Training on Electricity alone and Drones alone each reduces \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) on three test datasets, yet raises \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) above off-the-shelf levels in four of six cells in AI & offshoring (Figure 3). AI & offshoring is the test dataset that has a five-point rating scale for its outcome variable. These failures arise even though single-dataset training lowers CE in all 24 held-out test cells. A closer approximation of individual choices (i.e., lower CE) therefore does not guarantee more accurate treatment effects. Pooling both training datasets eliminates these failures, making unseen-study transfer more robust than training on either dataset alone.

Choice training also reduces error more reliably than scaling model parameters. Similar to the pattern seen for generative LLMs (Figure 1), scaling off-the-shelf decision models fails to improve treatment-effect recovery consistently. Interestingly, choice-trained KEV-0.8B outperforms off-the-shelf KEV-9B on three of the four test datasets, despite having an order of magnitude fewer parameters.

Note. \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) reports population OLS treatment-effect error \(\times 10^3\) (Equation 5). CE reports mean empirical cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood per choice task, in nats; Equation 2). Lower is better for both metrics. Bold marks the lowest value per model, dataset, and metric.

Attribute-level transfer across shared and unseen attributes

Because both training datasets include price, aggregate gains for the Consumer rights and AI & offshoring datasets could indicate that the models have learned only price-related information. However, attribute-level estimates rule out this explanation. On Consumer rights, pooled choice training lowers \(\mathrm{MSE}_\beta\) across the 34 non-price slopes by 23% to 41% at every model scale (Figure 4 and Table W6). Similarly, it reduces error across the 24 non-price job slopes on AI & offshoring, by 33% to 34% at every scale (Table W7). Because Food labels and Vaccines share no attributes with training, their gains likewise cannot stem from price information learned during training.

Despite these improvements, pooled choice training narrows but does not close the gap to human estimates. Some of the largest human effects remain attenuated or over-estimated at every model scale (Figure 4 and Figure 5). Full attribute-level estimates for the remaining datasets appear in the Web Appendix (Table W5, Table W9, Figure W3, and Table W8).

Conclusion

Off-the-shelf foundation models, whether generative LLMs or decision models, struggle to recover human treatment effects in unseen choice studies. Neither larger parameter counts nor higher reasoning effort reliably mitigates regression estimates. In this research, we show that pre-inference choice training of compact open-weight decision models provides consistent improvements. Training on only two historical choice studies lowers both OLS treatment-effect error and cross-entropy across every held-out test dataset and model scale. These gains extend to unseen attributes and new response formats without any target-study human data calibration.

Our results show how choice training fits alongside existing approaches for using AI models as synthetic respondents. Unlike post-inference calibration methods that require human data from the target study (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), choice training relies strictly on historical datasets and can serve as a stronger starting point before any statistical calibration (Hullman et al. 2026). It also generalizes beyond the domain of the training datasets and transfers across new domains and response formats. Finally, whereas large-scale behavioral models depend on hundreds of studies and autoregressive text generation (Binz et al. 2025, Kolluri et al. 2025), our approach achieves unseen-study transfer from two datasets using a compact model architecture.

For marketing researchers, these findings carry the following practical implications. Before any human data is collected, choice-trained decision models offer a stronger starting point that can support exploratory pre-testing and experimental design while potentially reducing the residual bias left for post-inference calibration. In addition, pooling various training datasets yields more dependable transfer than single-dataset training. Also, choice training reduces error more consistently than scaling model parameters. Because decision models also return exact choice probabilities without Monte Carlo sampling cost, these gains require far less compute than generative LLMs.

Our results also expose a limitation of training on cross-entropy alone. Single-dataset training lowers cross-entropy on every held-out test cell, yet, for some test datasets, it raises treatment-effect error. Lower cross-entropy therefore does not guarantee more accurate experimental slope estimation. One plausible reason is that cross-entropy weights every choice task equally without directly penalizing bias in treatment-versus-control contrasts. Because decision models yield exact, differentiable expected OLS slopes (Equation 4), future research can train jointly on respondent-level cross-entropy and a direct treatment-effect loss.

Moreover, because pooled training combines two datasets and more choice tasks than either single-dataset run, matched-volume ablations are needed to separate dataset diversity from data volume. Similarly, choice-training generative LLMs and comparing with decision models would isolate architectural advantages of decision models from training-data gains. On the estimation side, choice-trained models still attenuate or overshoot some of the largest human effects at every scale. Extending choice training to ordinal scales, non-US samples, and richer respondent covariates therefore offers a natural next step toward generating more general synthetic respondents.

Token serialization, query-key readout, and covariate mappings

Serialization. For each respondent, the covariates \(\mathbf{s}_t\) are rendered as a state block of “Label: Value” lines (Table W1), opened by a state marker. Each task context \(c_t\) then appears as a branch that begins with a question marker and the task instructions, places each of the \(K_t\) options between an option-start and an option-end marker, and ends with a decision marker (Figure W1). The five markers reuse reserved tokens from the backbone vocabulary, so the serialization introduces no new embeddings. Because every task branch attends to the same shared state block, several tasks for one respondent can be scored against a single encoded state.

Query-key readout. Let \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\mathrm{dec}}\) be the final-layer hidden state at the decision marker of task \(t\), and \(\mathbf{h}_{t,k}\) the hidden state at the option-end marker of option \(k\). The pointer head projects both into a \(d_p\)-dimensional space,

\[ \mathbf{a}_t = \mathbf{W}_Q \mathbf{h}_t^{\mathrm{dec}} + \mathbf{c}_Q, \qquad \mathbf{b}_{t,k} = \mathbf{W}_K \mathbf{h}_{t,k} + \mathbf{c}_K, \qquad z_{t,k}(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{b}_{t,k}^{\top} \mathbf{a}_t}{\sqrt{d_p}}, \tag{6}\]

with \(d_p = 256\), and Equation 1 turns the scores into choice probabilities. Because the same projections score each option-end marker separately and Equation 1 normalizes over the listed options, the head handles any number of options \(K_t\).

Note. Each covariate enters the input as one “Label: Value” line (Figure W1); covariates a respondent did not report are omitted. Country is not included.

Off-the-shelf generative LLM baseline protocol

We compare off-the-shelf generative LLMs and decision models on a random subsample of 200 Vaccines respondents (seed 42; 1,000 three-option tasks), matching the AI-only sample size of Wang et al. (2026). Each model’s choices enter the same OLS operator as the main analysis, and errors are computed against the full-sample human slopes (\(N = 1,971\); 11 slopes).

Prompt. Generative LLMs receive the prompt of Wang et al. (2026, sec. 5.2.1). The system prompt reads “You should act like a person with the following characteristics deciding whether or not to receive a COVID-19 vaccination:”, followed by one line per respondent covariate (e.g., “Age: 18-29”). The user prompt lists options A and B by efficacy, protective duration, major and minor side effects, authorization, origin, and endorsement, adds “C: You choose neither A nor B.”, and asks “Which option would you choose as this person? Your response should be a single letter A, B, or C.” Gemini prompts omit the respondent’s region, which the covariate mapping added after those runs.

Generation. We query Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (minimal and high thinking) and Gemini 4 Argon (low and high thinking) through the Gemini API with default sampling. We serve Qwen3.5-0.8B, 4B, and 9B with vLLM in bfloat16, toggling reasoning through the chat template and using Qwen’s official recommended sampling settings (reasoning off: temperature 0.7, top-\(p\) 0.8; reasoning on: temperature 1.0, top-\(p\) 0.95; both top-\(k\) 20 and presence penalty 1.5) without an output-length limit. Each generative LLM produces one response per task.

Parsing. A response counts as a choice only if, after removing surrounding whitespace, it is exactly “A”, “B”, or “C”; all other responses are counted as refusals and excluded from the regression. Valid responses enter the regression as one-hot choice vectors.

Decision models. Off-the-shelf KEV-0.8B, KEV-4B, and KEV-9B receive the same respondent covariates and choice task in their native serialization and return a probability over the three options in one forward pass, which enters the regression directly. Following the released model cards, these baseline evaluations apply each checkpoint’s shipped calibration temperature (2.35, 2.41, and 2.19, respectively).

Note. All models condition on respondent demographics over the same 200-respondent, 1,000-task subsample of Vaccines (Kreps et al. 2020), evaluated against the full-sample human OLS benchmark (\(N = 1,971\)). Refusals count tasks where a generative LLM failed to return a valid choice option. Opt-out reports the share (or expected probability for decision models) of selecting neither vaccine. Values back Figure 1.
