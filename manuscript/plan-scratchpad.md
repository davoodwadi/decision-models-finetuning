## Section 1: Introduction (`#sec-intro`)

Simulating consumer choices, attitudes, and survey responses with foundation models---commonly referred to as silicon sampling---has attracted growing interest in marketing, public opinion, and behavioral research [@argyle2023out; @horton2023large; @brand2023using; @goli2024frontiers; @sarstedt2024using; @leng2026reducing; @wang2026large]. In marketing applications, the primary goal is often downstream econometric inference, such as estimating part-worth utilities in conjoint analysis or causal treatment effects in randomized experiments. Currently, researchers rely on generative large language models (LLMs) that produce text one token at a time. However, naively replacing human respondents with LLM choices introduces severe bias in downstream regressions [@goli2024frontiers; @leng2026reducing; @wang2026large]. Because generative LLMs are post-trained for deterministic reasoning and safety compliance, scaling up model size or enabling chain-of-thought (`CoT`) reasoning makes their choices overly rational [@wang2026large]. This collapses human choice diversity, triggers safety refusals on sensitive topics, and worsens regression parameter recovery. Furthermore, estimating choice shares from text generation requires costly Monte Carlo sampling [@wadi2025monte; @zahradnickova2026ai]. Consequently, recent studies conclude that raw LLM outputs cannot directly replace human data, and instead collect primary human responses in the target domain to train post-hoc data-augmentation or sample-calibration estimators [@wang2026large; @leng2026reducing].

Decision models, also known as System One architectures [@palmer2026kev; @deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev; @geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple; @li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision], offer a direct alternative for silicon sampling. Instead of generating text tokens, a decision model takes a respondent persona and a structured question---such as a categorical choice task or an ordinal Likert scale---and outputs a normalized probability distribution over the supplied options in a single prefill forward pass. Because the model scores each option directly from its contextualized representation and restricts probability mass strictly to the valid options, it eliminates text-generation variance, Monte Carlo sampling overhead, and safety refusals. Moreover, its continuous choice probabilities can be directly plugged into maximum likelihood and ordinary least squares regressions.

Despite this architectural advantage, off-the-shelf decision models---similar to generative LLMs---suffer from a fundamental mismatch between their training objective and the goal of silicon sampling. Both model families are trained to maximize single-answer accuracy on objective benchmarks such as mathematics, code verification, and factual question answering [@palmer2026kev; @geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple; @gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale]. Standard training pairs each prompt with a single class label and minimizes cross-entropy loss to push all probability mass onto that one winning option. In consumer and social surveys, however, respondents who share a demographic profile never agree unanimously. As shown in @fig-gender-mixed across representative five-point Likert items from US survey data, both male and female respondents spread their answers across all five rating levels. A model trained to concentrate probability mass on a single answer treats genuine within-group behavioral variation as classification error, producing overconfident probabilities and biased downstream regression estimates.

To resolve this training mismatch, we fine-tune open-weight decision models [@palmer2026kev], built on Qwen 3.5 backbones [@qwen3.5], directly on respondent-level survey microdata and test whether this training transfers zero-shot to downstream econometric inference. Our training corpus combines 10 waves of the US International Social Survey Programme (ISSP 2015 to 2024; $14,356$ respondents and 616 questions) with the Twin-2K-500 US panel [$2,058$ respondents and 974 questions; @toubia2025twin], totaling $1,881,950$ individual respondent-question observations across 1,572 training questions and 17 harmonized demographic and attitudinal attributes. Using grouped shared-prefix training, we encode each respondent's multi-attribute persona once and minimize cross-entropy loss across their packed individual responses. This trains the decision model to predict calibrated conditional choice distributions at unit temperature ($T = 1$), requiring no post-hoc temperature tuning and no target-domain human calibration data.

We evaluate off-the-shelf and fine-tuned decision models across held-out survey microdata and two out-of-domain marketing regression benchmarks. First, on $13,183$ respondent-question records from 18 held-out ISSP and Twin-2K-500 validation questions, fine-tuning reduces respondent-level cross-entropy by $25.2\%$ (from $2.398$ to $1.793$ bits for Kev 9B). Second, we follow the empirical benchmark and Multinomial Logit (MNL) evaluation protocol of @wang2026large on the @kreps2020factors COVID-19 vaccine conjoint dataset ($N = 1,971$ respondents and $9,855$ choice sets across $d = 11$ attribute levels). We evaluate parameter recovery (`MAPE` and `MSE` against full-sample human $\boldsymbol{\beta}^*$) on a $1,000$-choice-set subsample under both @wang2026large's random-persona prompt and actual respondent personas. Consistent with @wang2026large, generative LLMs (`Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite` and `Gemini 4 Argon`) exhibit severe estimation error ($\mathrm{MSE} = 0.59$ to $26.22$, $\mathrm{MAPE} = 203.6\%$ to $1507.7\%$) that worsens at higher thinking budgets and is compounded by safety refusals. Off-the-shelf `Kev 9B` ($\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0932$ to $0.0982$) already outperforms the generative LLMs by an order of magnitude. Fine-tuned `Kev 9B FT` reduces MNL MSE by a further $76\%$ to $78\%$, reaching $\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0205$ ($\mathrm{MAPE} = 51.8\%$) under random personas and $\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0239$ ($\mathrm{MAPE} = 56.6\%$) under respondent personas---approaching the finite-sample error of the 200-respondent human subsample itself ($\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0065$, $\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.1\%$). Third, we test zero-shot generalization to a randomized clinical trial of front-of-package nutrition labels [$N = 5,633$ US grocery shoppers and $33,798$ product healthfulness choices across 6 labeling arms; @grummon2025nutrition]. Fine-tuning substantially reduces respondent-level choice cross-entropy and accurately recovers the causal treatment-arm effects in participant-level OLS regressions, even when conditioning on consumer nutrition attributes never seen during training.

## Section 2: Related Work (`#sec-related`)

### Silicon sampling and econometric inference with generative LLMs

Early work in silicon sampling prompted generative LLMs with demographic backstories to simulate human responses in political, economic, and marketing surveys [@argyle2023out; @horton2023large; @brand2023using; @goli2024frontiers; @sarstedt2024using; @pmlr-v202-santurkar23a; @durmus2024towards]. Although generative LLMs often capture the direction of average preferences, prior studies document systematic discrepancies between LLM samples and human populations. Common failure modes include mode collapse onto a single option, flattened within-group variance, demographic stereotyping, and sensitivity to prompt formatting [@pmlr-v202-santurkar23a; @shu-etal-2024-dont; @koo2024benchmarking; @kabir-etal-2025-break; @deas-mckeown-2025-artificial; @jung-etal-2026-psychometric; @wadi2025monte; @zahradnickova2026ai]. In marketing applications, these distortions cause severe parameter bias when LLM responses are used for downstream econometric estimation [@goli2024frontiers; @leng2026reducing; @wang2026large]. In choice-based conjoint analysis, @wang2026large show that relying solely on AI choices or naively pooling them with human data biases Multinomial Logit (MNL) part-worth estimates. They also show that more capable LLMs and chain-of-thought (`CoT`) prompting worsen estimation error by making choices overly rational.

To mitigate these biases, recent marketing studies propose post-hoc statistical corrections that combine LLM outputs with target-domain human data. Building on prediction-powered inference [@angelopoulos2023prediction; @angelopoulos2023ppi++], @wang2026large develop an AI-augmented estimator (AAE). Their method uses a primary sample of human conjoint responses to learn a de-biasing transformation over auxiliary LLM choices. Similarly, @leng2026reducing propose Human Mimicry Calibration (HuMCal), which learns optimal weights to combine LLM outputs across prompts, personas, or checkpoints using human calibration benchmarks. In natural language processing, recent studies fine-tune causal language models on aggregated group survey distributions [@suh-etal-2025-language; @huang-etal-2026-distribution; @ku2026siliconsamplingcrosssurveytransfer]. Our work complements these approaches in two ways. First, we shift from autoregressive text generation to decision models that directly output option probability distributions. Second, we show that fine-tuning on respondent-level microdata from general surveys transfers zero-shot to conjoint MNL and randomized trial OLS regressions without target-domain human calibration data.

### Decision models and direct probability readouts

To avoid the cost and sampling variance of free-text generation, several studies extract next-token probabilities over option labels from causal LLMs [@kadavath2022language; @kuribayashi-etal-2024-psychometric; @bentegeac2025token; @wadi2026every]. Decision models extend this idea into a dedicated single-pass architecture [@palmer2026kev; @deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev; @geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple; @li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision; @asseri2026calibratedwhompersonalanguage; @li2026kitescalingjevpopulation]. Built on pretrained language model backbones [@qwen3.5], decision models compile a context state and typed questions (`score` for ordered rating scales and `choice` for categorical options) into a structured prompt. They then compute option probabilities in one prefill pass. Vocabulary-letter readouts map options to arbitrary token symbols (such as "A", "B", and "C") and remain susceptible to token-frequency priors. By contrast, a decision model scores each option directly from its contextualized text representation and restricts probability mass strictly to the supplied options [@palmer2026kev]. However, existing decision models are trained on single-label objective benchmarks. As a result, they exhibit severe probability compression on subjective and ordinal tasks [@gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale], which motivates our respondent-level training framework.

## Section 3: Methodology (`#sec-methodology`) — Proposed Flow & Draft Prose

### 3.1 Predicting respondent-level choice probabilities

Consider a survey or experimental dataset of human respondents indexed by $i \in {1, \dots, N}$. Each respondent has a multi-attribute persona state $\mathbf{s}*i$ (such as sex, age, education, income, political party, and race) and answers a set of questions $\mathcal{Q}i$. Each question $q \in \mathcal{Q}i$ presents $K\_q$ discrete answer options $\mathcal{Y}q = {y\_1, \dots, y{K\_q}}$. For categorical `choice` items, the options are unordered alternatives that may include attribute descriptions. For ordinal `score` items, the options are ordered rating levels $y\_1 \prec y\_2 \prec \dots \prec y{K\_q}$. Let $\mathbf{y}{i,q} = (y*{i,q,1}, \dots, y\_{i,q,K\_q}) \in {0, 1}^{K\_q}$ denote respondent $i$'s observed one-hot choice vector on question $q$, where $y\_{i,q,k} = 1$ if respondent $i$ selected option $y\_k$ and $0$ otherwise.

In a decision model [@palmer2026kev], the persona state $\mathbf{s}*i$, question text $q$, and options $\mathcal{Y}q$ are formatted into a structured prompt and processed in one causal forward pass. Let $\mathbf{h}{i,q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d*{\mathrm{model}}}$ denote the final-layer hidden state at the closing token of question $q$. Let $\mathbf{h}*{i,q,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d*{\mathrm{model}}}$ denote the final-layer hidden state at the closing token of option $k$. Using learned linear projections $\mathbf{W}*q, \mathbf{W}k \in \mathbb{R}^{d{\mathrm{head}} \times d*{\mathrm{model}}}$, the decision head computes a score $z\_{i,q,k}$ for each option $k$: $$
z_{i,q,k} = \frac{(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}_{i,q})^\top (\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{i,q,k})}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}.
$$ {#eq-pointer-head} Applying a softmax function over the $K_q$ option scores yields the predicted choice distribution in a single pass: $$
P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}_i) = \frac{\exp(z_{i,q,k} / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K_q} \exp(z_{i,q,j} / T)},
$$ {#eq-model-pmf} where $T$ is the readout temperature ($T = 1$ for all fine-tuned models, with no post-hoc temperature fitting).

### 3.2 Respondent-level training and grouped shared-prefix packing

To train a decision model to approximate human choice behavior conditional on a persona, we optimize model parameters $\theta$ directly on individual respondent-question observations $(i, q)$. Specifically, we minimize respondent-level cross-entropy loss (measured in bits) against the observed one-hot response $\mathbf{y}_{i,q}$: $$
\mathcal{L}_{i,q}(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_q} y_{i,q,k} \log_2 P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}_i; \theta).
$$ {#eq-resp-ce} By the law of total expectation, averaging $\mathcal{L}_{i,q}(\theta)$ across all respondents who share persona state $\mathbf{s}$ equals the cross-entropy between the empirical conditional human distribution $P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s})$ and $P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta)$. Minimizing @eq-resp-ce is therefore equivalent to minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}) \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta))$ up to the fixed entropy of the human responses. Questions on which respondents with the same persona naturally disagree are thus learned as spread probability distributions rather than single-class labels.

In respondent-level survey data, each participant $i$ typically answers dozens of questions $\mathcal{Q}\_i$ while sharing the same persona state $\mathbf{s}*i$. Encoding $\mathbf{s}i$ separately for every question is computationally redundant. To accelerate training and evaluation, we use grouped shared-prefix packing. We partition each respondent's questions into groups of up to $M{\max}$ questions (bounded by a maximum character budget $C*{\max}$). The model encodes the persona prefix $\mathbf{s}\_i$ once and scores all questions in the group against the cached state prefix in parallel. During training, we randomly permute the order of demographic attributes in $\mathbf{s}\_i$ for each group so that the model is invariant to attribute ordering.

### 3.3 Downstream econometric estimation from model predictions

To evaluate whether decision models can replace human respondents in applied marketing research, we feed the models' predicted choice probabilities directly into the econometric regressions of two out-of-domain studies.

#### Multinomial Logit (MNL) in conjoint analysis

We follow the choice-based conjoint setup and evaluation protocol of @wang2026large. Each respondent $i$ evaluates $t \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ paired choice tasks. Each task presents two hypothetical product profiles (Option A and Option B) with binary attribute vectors $\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}, \mathbf{x}_{i,t,B} \in \{0, 1\}^d$, alongside an outside no-choice option (Option C) whose utility is normalized to zero. Under the standard MNL specification [@wang2026large], the choice probabilities for part-worth parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are: $$
P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}, \qquad P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})},
$$ {#eq-mnl-probs} and $P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) - P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta})$. Given observed or predicted choice shares $(p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})$ across $n$ choice sets, we estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ by minimizing the negative log-likelihood via Newton-CG: $$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d} -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{(i,t)} \Big[ p_{i,t,A} \log P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) + p_{i,t,B} \log P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) + p_{i,t,C} \log P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) \Big].
$$ {#eq-mnl-mle} For human respondents and generative LLMs, $(p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})$ is a one-hot choice vector. For decision models, we use the continuous choice probabilities output by @eq-model-pmf. Following @wang2026large, let $\boldsymbol{\beta}^*$ denote the ground-truth MNL parameter vector estimated on the full human dataset. We evaluate parameter recovery using Mean Absolute Percentage Error ($\mathrm{MAPE}$) and Mean Squared Error ($\mathrm{MSE}$) across the $d$ attributes: $$
\mathrm{MAPE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum\_{j=1}^{d} \frac{|\hat{\beta}\_j - \beta^j|}{|\beta^\_j|} \times 100%, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^) = \frac{1}{d} \sum*{j=1}^{d} (\hat{\beta}\_j - \beta^\*\_j)^2.
$$ {#eq-wang-metrics}

#### Participant-level OLS in randomized clinical trials

In our second econometric study, we evaluate causal treatment-effect recovery in the front-of-package nutrition labeling randomized clinical trial of @grummon2025nutrition. Each shopper $i$ is randomized to one of six labeling arms $a_i$ (the reference `Positive` arm or one of five treatment arms) and completes six 3-option product healthfulness choice tasks $q \in \{1, \dots, 6\}$. In each task, option $y_1$ is the objectively healthier food or beverage product. For each shopper $i$, we compute the mean probability of selecting the healthier product across the six categories, $\bar{p}_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{q=1}^6 P(Y_{i,q} = y_1 \mid \mathbf{s}_i, a_i)$. Following @grummon2025nutrition, we estimate the five treatment-arm effects $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^5$ relative to the `Positive` reference arm via ordinary least squares (OLS) with HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. We compare the model-estimated treatment effects $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ against the human RCT treatment effects $\boldsymbol{\beta}^*$ using $\mathrm{OLS\ MAPE}$, $\mathrm{OLS\ MSE}$, $\mathrm{OLS\ MAE}$, and Spearman rank correlation $\rho$.
