\subsection{Silicon sampling, persona prompting, and System 2
drift}\label{silicon-sampling-persona-prompting-and-system-2-drift}

Consumer and marketing researchers increasingly explore silicon samples,
defined as AI-generated synthetic respondents used to approximate human
survey responses (Argyle et al., 2023; Arora et al., 2025; Sarstedt et
al., 2024). Early work demonstrated that generative large language
models (LLMs) conditioned on demographic backstories can sometimes
mirror broad patterns of political attitudes and consumer preferences
(Argyle et al., 2023). However, systematic evidence across behavioral
and marketing domains reveals inconsistent correspondence with human
samples (Goli \& Singh, 2024; Sarstedt et al., 2024). Reviewing 28
empirical comparisons, Sarstedt et al. (2024) conclude that silicon
samples from off-the-shelf generative LLMs are currently better suited
to upstream qualitative pretesting than to quantitative main studies.

A central reason for this gap is that modern generative LLMs have
shifted progressively toward deliberative System 2 processing. As
Hagendorff et al. (2023) show and Sarstedt et al. (2024) highlight,
while early language models exhibited human-like intuitive System 1
patterns, newer instruction-tuned and chain-of-thought models behave
like System 2 rational agents chasing a single defensible answer.
Trained on massive web corpora without a clearly defined human
population and shaped by safety guardrails, these models compress
interpersonal variability, exhibit a deterministic correct-answer
effect, and flatten minority or subgroup viewpoints (Santurkar et al.,
2023; Sarstedt et al., 2024).

Crucially, recent evidence in Psychology \& Marketing shows that
supplying accurate demographic personas in the prompt does not resolve
these distortions. Zahradnickova et al. (2026) demonstrate that even
when a frontier generative LLM receives official population
distributions in its context or is conditioned directly on the exact
demographic profiles of human survey respondents (Study 2 and Study 4,
Table 5; Zahradnickova et al., 2026), its attitudinal responses still
diverge sharply from human benchmarks and collapse variance.
Consequently, the limitations of persona-prompted silicon sampling stem
not merely from missing demographic context, but from a deeper mismatch
between generative language modeling and respondent-level distributional
simulation.

\subsection{Architecture dependence and System One decision
models}\label{architecture-dependence-and-system-one-decision-models}

To evaluate synthetic respondents systematically, Zahradnickova et al.
(2026) conceptualize distributional fidelity across four levels, namely
univariate fidelity (marginal distributions and dispersion),
multivariate fidelity (joint variable structure), subgroup fidelity
(preservation within demographic subpopulations), and semantic fidelity
(open-ended text). Their central thesis is that distributional fidelity
in silicon sampling is not architecture-neutral. Comparing generative
LLMs against a proprietary neuro-symbolic AI system (Lakmoos AI) across
demographic benchmarks in Czechia, Germany, and France and a
315-respondent Czech housing survey, Zahradnickova et al. (2026) show
that separating structured statistical representation from free-form
text generation yields markedly closer alignment with human
distributions.

Yet this architectural turn leaves two critical gaps for consumer and
marketing researchers. First, both systems evaluated by Zahradnickova et
al. (2026) are proprietary black boxes, restricting transparency,
independent auditing, and exact reproducibility. Second, their empirical
test of subgroup fidelity evaluates census demographic headcounts
(gender by age) rather than attitudinal differences across segments,
while their attitudinal benchmark spans six closed-ended items in a
single narrow domain. Whether an open, generalizable architecture can
recover between-group attitudinal contrasts across diverse unseen survey
topics remains an open empirical question.

Decision models offer a distinct, open-weight architectural alternative
explicitly designed as System One models (Deußer et al., 2026; Geuter et
al., 2026; Palmer, 2026). Instead of generating text tokens
sequentially, a decision model replaces a language model's
text-generation head with a pointer head that scores candidate options
directly in a single forward pass. This design produces exact analytical
probability distributions over valid survey responses, eliminating Monte
Carlo sampling noise, temperature tuning, safety refusals, and the
duplicate-persona redundancy that degrades effective sample size in
generative protocols (Zahradnickova et al., 2026). Even so, a System One
architecture alone is not sufficient. Because off-the-shelf decision
models are pretrained on objective single-label benchmarks, they possess
the fast inference structure of System 1 without calibrated human social
priors, leading to ordinal-scale distortion and cultural miscalibration
prior to survey training (Asseri \& Asseri, 2026; Gao et al., 2026).

\subsection{From prompting personas to learning
personas}\label{from-prompting-personas-to-learning-personas}

A growing stream of research therefore moves beyond off-the-shelf
prompting by adapting models with human behavioral or survey data,
spanning three distinct paradigms. Post-inference calibration and
data-augmentation methods adjust fixed model outputs to reduce
econometric bias, yet they require collecting human responses from the
target study itself (Leng et al., 2026; M. Wang et al., 2026).
Individual-level digital twins capture rich personal heterogeneity but
require extensive prior questionnaires from each target individual
(Toubia et al., 2025), whereas cognitive foundation models trained on
behavioral task trials lack demographic persona structure for
population-level sampling ({Binz et al.}, 2025). As Zahradnickova et al.
(2026) note, benchmarking population-level trained models that
generalize without target-study data remains open future work.

Pre-inference survey training addresses this gap by updating model
weights directly on historical human survey microdata so the model
learns how demographic attributes map onto response distributions on
unseen questions (Huang et al., 2026; Kolluri et al., 2025; Ku et al.,
2026; Suh et al., 2025; S. Wang et al., 2025). In consumer and marketing
research, however, training models on demographic group data raises an
explicit theoretical tension. Whereas off-the-shelf persona prompting
tends to flatten demographic differences and compress within-group
variance, Sarstedt et al. (2024) draw on Santurkar et al. (2023) to warn
that training models to mimic group responses may backfire in the
opposite direction by pushing models to embody exaggerated caricatures
of those groups or by failing to generalize beyond the training survey.

Table~\ref{tbl-lit-positioning} summarizes where our research stands
relative to these existing silicon-sampling paradigms. Because
open-weight Kev decision models (Palmer, 2026) are built directly on
pretrained Qwen3.5 backbones, we can isolate explicit System 2
deliberation (Qwen3.5 with reasoning enabled versus disabled), System
One pointer-head architecture alone (off-the-shelf Kev), and learned
demographic priors (survey-trained Kev) on the exact same foundation
backbone across matched parameter scales. Training System One decision
models on large-scale persona-rich human surveys (ISSP and Twin-2K-500)
and evaluating zero-shot transfer on unseen multi-topic questions across
eight demographic dimensions (Suh et al., 2025) directly tests whether
learned System 1 personas recover between-group attitudinal differences
and within-group dispersion without collapsing into flattening or
caricature.
