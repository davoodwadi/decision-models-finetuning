# Positioning for the Psychology & Marketing paper

## Target journal and literature anchor

This paper targets Psychology & Marketing and positions itself directly inside the journal's growing literature on silicon sampling in consumer and marketing research. Prior work in Psychology & Marketing has established "silicon samples" as the standard term for AI-generated synthetic respondents and has focused primarily on persona-prompted generative large language models (LLMs). Sarstedt et al. (2024) reviewed 28 silicon-sampling studies and found highly mixed correspondence with human samples across domains. They diagnosed two structural limitations of off-the-shelf LLMs, namely that web training corpora represent no well-defined human population and that newer LLMs shift away from intuitive human System 1 subjectivity toward deliberative System 2 "correct answer" determinism (citing Hagendorff et al., 2023). Sarstedt et al. (2024) also raised an explicit warning about training models on group data, cautioning (via Santurkar et al., 2023) that fine-tuning may swing too far and push models to embody caricatures of demographic groups or fail to generalize beyond the training survey.

More recently, Zahradnickova et al. (2026) introduced a four-level distributional fidelity framework in Psychology & Marketing covering univariate, multivariate, subgroup, and semantic fidelity, and argued that distributional fidelity in silicon sampling is not architecture-neutral. Comparing generative LLMs (GPT-4o and GPT-4 Turbo) against a proprietary neuro-symbolic AI system (Lakmoos AI), they demonstrated that prompted generative LLMs systematically compress variance and flatten demographic subgroups. Crucially, their Study 4 control analysis (Table 5) showed that even when GPT is conditioned on exact human demographic profiles, it still diverges severely from human attitudinal distributions, confirming that persona prompting alone cannot solve architectural and training-objective mismatches. Yet Zahradnickova et al. (2026) left three major gaps open for future research. Both systems they evaluated are closed proprietary black boxes, their attitudinal evaluation spanned only six closed-ended items in a single Czech co-housing domain without testing between-group attitudinal contrasts across demographic segments, and they explicitly left population-level trained models (distinct from individual digital twins such as Toubia et al., 2025 and non-demographic cognitive models such as Binz et al., 2025) as open future work.

## System 1 versus System 2 framing

The System 1 versus System 2 distinction provides a direct psychological lens for Zahradnickova et al.'s (2026) architecture-alignment thesis and builds directly on Sarstedt et al.'s (2024) observation that modern LLMs have shifted from intuitive System 1 patterns toward deliberative System 2 processing (citing Hagendorff et al., 2023). Modern generative and reasoning LLMs deliberate token-by-token, optimize for verbal coherence and a single defensible answer, and pass outputs through normative safety filters. When prompted to roleplay a demographic persona, System 2 deliberation creates a double risk, either flattening demographic differences through balanced rationalization or caricaturing them through explicit textbook stereotyping.

Decision models (e.g., Jev and Kev) are explicitly designed and named as System One architectures (Deusser et al., 2026; Geuter et al., 2026; Palmer, 2026). Rather than generating text tokens, a decision model attaches a pointer head to a pretrained language backbone and maps a persona and question directly to an exact analytical probability distribution over valid response options in a single forward pass, eliminating Monte Carlo sampling noise, temperature heuristics, safety refusals, and the duplicate-persona redundancy documented by Zahradnickova et al. (2026). Crucially, however, a System One architecture alone is not sufficient. Because off-the-shelf decision models are pretrained on objective single-label benchmarks, they possess the fast inference mechanism of System 1 without calibrated human social priors (Gao et al., 2026; Asseri & Asseri, 2026). Training a System One decision model on large-scale human survey microdata populates that fast architecture with empirical demographic priors, testing whether learned System 1 representations recover real demographic heterogeneity without collapsing into either flattening or caricature.

## Separation from the companion Marketing Science paper

This manuscript is designed as a distinct companion to our Marketing Science paper in [manuscript-training.qmd](../sim-respondents/manuscript-training.qmd) with clean boundaries across research question, training data, evaluation estimands, and journal terminology. The Marketing Science paper investigates econometric causal inference in experimental marketing, asking whether choice-trained decision models trained on discrete choice and conjoint experiments can recover ordinary least squares (OLS) attribute slopes and causal treatment effects on held-out conjoint and randomized experiments. That paper intentionally adopts Marketing Science conventions ("synthetic respondents" and "synthetic choice data") and focuses on within-study experimental treatment effects.

In contrast, this Psychology & Marketing paper examines demographic persona learning for silicon sampling in survey and consumer attitude research. Instead of training on conjoint experiments to recover experimental attribute slopes, we train decision models on persona-rich cross-sectional and multi-topic human surveys, and we evaluate whether the trained models recover subgroup response distributions and between-group demographic differences on completely unseen survey questions. Consistent with Psychology & Marketing norms (Sarstedt et al., 2024; Zahradnickova et al., 2026), this manuscript uses the terms "silicon sampling" and "silicon samples" throughout.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Target journal | Marketing Science | Psychology & Marketing |
| Literature framing | Synthetic respondents for econometric inference | Silicon sampling, distributional fidelity, and System 1 versus System 2 persona learning |
| Core research question | Can choice-trained decision models recover causal treatment effects and OLS slopes? | Can survey-trained System One decision models learn demographic personas and recover subgroup differences? |

## Backbone-matched experimental decomposition

Because Kev decision models are built directly on pretrained Qwen3.5 backbones across matched parameter scales (0.8B, 4B, and 9B), we can run a clean backbone-matched comparison that avoids the cross-vendor confounds present in prior proprietary benchmarks (e.g., comparing GPT-4o against a separate proprietary neuro-symbolic pipeline). Evaluating four progressive regimes on the exact same Qwen3.5 pretrained family isolates each component of our dual-process hypothesis.

| Experimental regime | Cognitive and architectural role | What the adjacent contrast isolates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qwen3.5 (reasoning ON) | Explicit System 2 deliberative generation | Baseline deliberative chain-of-thought silicon sampling |
| Qwen3.5 (reasoning OFF) | Direct generative LLM without chain-of-thought | Effect of removing explicit System 2 deliberation while keeping generative next-token prediction |
| Kev off-the-shelf | System One pointer-head architecture only | Effect of switching from generative token prediction to direct System One option scoring without survey training |
| Kev survey-trained | System One architecture with learned demographic priors | Effect of learning demographic personas from human survey microdata on top of the System One architecture |

All paper-specific datasets and loaders reside in [code/papers/pm/src/data/](../../code/papers/pm/src/data). For training, we draw on broad, persona-rich survey datasets such as ISSP (waves from 2015 to 2024), Twin-2K-500, and the Twin-2K-500 Mega-Study, with optional expansion to additional cleaned survey waves as needed. Because silicon-sampling studies condition on both single demographic segments and richer multi-attribute profiles, training can incorporate partial-persona marginalization as implemented in [marginals.py](../../code/papers/pm/src/data/marginals.py), exposing the model to empirical response distributions conditioned on subsets of demographic traits as well as full personas.

For held-out evaluation, we test zero-shot transfer to unseen survey questions that never appear in training, anchored by the SubPOP benchmark in [code/papers/pm/src/data/subpop/](../../code/papers/pm/src/data/subpop). SubPOP evaluates subgroup response distributions across eight demographic dimensions, namely political party, political ideology, sex, race, religion, education, income, and region. The decisive test is not merely whether the model matches overall population averages, which can be satisfied without understanding who is answering. Instead, we upgrade Zahradnickova et al.'s (2026) subgroup fidelity from demographic headcounts to attitudinal subgroup contrasts, testing whether the model accurately recovers demographic differences between groups on novel topics (e.g., whether Republicans express less feminist attitudes than Democrats, or how religious, gender, racial, educational, and income subgroups diverge in direction, ordering, and magnitude) while preserving realistic within-group response variance and avoiding both flattening and stereotype caricature.

## Intended contributions

First, this paper advances the architecture-dependence debate on silicon sampling in Psychology & Marketing through a dual-process (System 1 versus System 2) lens. By benchmarking Qwen3.5 (reasoning ON and OFF) against backbone-matched open-weight Kev decision models (off-the-shelf and survey-trained), we cleanly separate the effects of explicit System 2 deliberation, System One pointer-head scoring, and survey-trained demographic priors without proprietary black-box confounds.

Second, this paper shifts the methodological paradigm from persona prompting to persona learning. Directly answering Zahradnickova et al.'s (2026) finding that supplying exact demographics in a prompt fails to restore attitudinal fidelity, we test whether updating System One model weights on public human survey microdata with partial-trait marginalization teaches generalizable persona representations that transfer zero-shot to unseen survey topics and response scales.

Third, this paper upgrades subgroup distributional fidelity to between-group attitudinal contrast recovery and resolves the flattening-versus-caricature tension raised by Sarstedt et al. (2024). Measuring signed demographic gaps, subgroup rank ordering, contrast magnitude calibration, and within-group dispersion across eight demographic axes directly tests whether trained silicon samples faithfully represent consumer segment heterogeneity or swing into exaggerated stereotyping.

Fourth, this paper provides open, reproducible methodology and practical guidelines for consumer and marketing researchers extending Sarstedt et al. (2024) and Zahradnickova et al. (2026). Analyzing performance across model scales, reasoning modes, demographic dimensions, and training data compositions clarifies when survey-trained System One silicon samples can reliably capture segment differences and where systematic biases remain.
