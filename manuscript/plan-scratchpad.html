Methodology

Decision model architecture and choice probabilities. Consider a survey or experimental dataset of human respondents indexed by \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Each respondent possesses a multi-attribute persona state \(\mathbf{s}_i\) (e.g., sex, age, education, income, political party, and race) and answers a set of questions \(\mathcal{Q}_i\). Each question \(q \in \mathcal{Q}_i\) presents \(K_q\) discrete answer options \(\mathcal{Y}_q = \{y_1, \dots, y_{K_q}\}\). For categorical choice items, the options are unordered alternatives; for ordinal score items, the options are ordered rating levels \(y_1 \prec y_2 \prec \dots \prec y_{K_q}\). Let \(\mathbf{y}_{i,q} = (y_{i,q,1}, \dots, y_{i,q,K_q}) \in \{0, 1\}^{K_q}\) denote respondent \(i\)’s observed one-hot response on question \(q\), where \(y_{i,q,k} = 1\) if respondent \(i\) selected option \(y_k\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Given a conditioning persona profile \(\mathbf{s}\), a decision model [@palmer2026kev] processes the prompt in one causal forward pass without a vocabulary generation head. The prompt places \(\mathbf{s}\) in a state segment, followed by the question stem \(q\), each option \(y_k\) enclosed in dedicated option delimiters, and a terminal decision token appended after the final option. Because the terminal decision token follows all \(K_q\) options, its final-layer hidden state \(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q(\mathbf{s}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) causally attends to the persona, the question stem, and the full option set. Let \(\mathbf{h}_{q,k}(\mathbf{s}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) denote the final-layer hidden state at the closing delimiter of option \(k\). Using learned affine projections \((\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{b}_q)\) and \((\mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{b}_k)\) with \(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}} \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) and \(\mathbf{b}_q, \mathbf{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}}}\), the decision head computes a scalar score \(z_{q,k}(\mathbf{s})\) for each option \(k\): \[ z_{q,k}(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{\big(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q(\mathbf{s}) + \mathbf{b}_q\big)^\top \big(\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{q,k}(\mathbf{s}) + \mathbf{b}_k\big)}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}. \tag{1}\] Applying a softmax function over the \(K_q\) scores yields the normalized choice distribution in a single pass: \[ P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta) = \frac{\exp(z_{q,k}(\mathbf{s}) / T)}{\sum_{m=1}^{K_q} \exp(z_{q,m}(\mathbf{s}) / T)}, \tag{2}\] where \(T\) denotes the readout temperature.

Empirical sample-distribution training. Rather than training on isolated one-hot responses, we supervise model parameters \(\theta\) directly against empirical sample probability distributions constructed across subsets of persona attributes. Let \(|\mathbf{s}_i|\) denote the number of recorded attributes in a respondent’s full persona \(\mathbf{s}_i\). At each training epoch, we draw a subset size \(\kappa\) uniformly from a schedule \(\mathcal{K} = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \text{"full"}, \text{"full"})\). Setting \(\ell = |\mathbf{s}_i|\) when \(\kappa = \text{"full"}\) and \(\ell = \min(\kappa, |\mathbf{s}_i|)\) otherwise, we sample \(\ell\) attributes without replacement from \(\mathbf{s}_i\) to form a conditioning sub-persona \(\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)} \subseteq \mathbf{s}_i\). Let \(\mathcal{I}(q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}) = \{j : \mathbf{s}_j \supseteq \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)},\; q \in \mathcal{Q}_j\}\) denote all training respondents who match \(\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}\) and answered question \(q\), with sample size \(n_{q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}} = |\mathcal{I}(q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)})| \ge 1\). Aggregating their observed choices yields the empirical sample probability distribution \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{q \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}} = (\hat{p}_{q,1 \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}}, \dots, \hat{p}_{q,K_q \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}})\): \[ \hat{p}_{q,k \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}} = \frac{1}{n_{q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{I}(q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)})} y_{j,q,k}, \qquad k \in \{1, \dots, K_q\}. \tag{3}\] We optimize \(\theta\) by minimizing the distributional cross-entropy loss against \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{q \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}}\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{q, \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}}(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_q} \hat{p}_{q,k \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}} \log_2 P_{\mathrm{model}}\!\left(Y_q = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}; \theta\right). \tag{4}\] Up to the constant entropy of \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{q \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}}\), minimizing Equation 4 directly minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{q \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}} \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}(\cdot \mid \mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}; \theta)\right)\). Coarse sub-personas (\(\ell \le 5\)) pool multiple matching respondents to supply low-variance empirical choice shares, while full-persona draws (\(\ell = |\mathbf{s}_i|\)) preserve fine-grained individual conditioning. Sampling across \(\mathcal{K}\) also trains the model to marginalize consistently when downstream studies provide partial persona profiles at test time.

Survey Training and Validation

Training Corpus and Model Configuration

Survey microdata and question split. We construct a harmonized training corpus from two public US survey microdata sources (Table 1). The first comprises \(10\) consecutive US waves of the International Social Survey Programme (US ISSP, 2015 to 2024; @apptbl-issp-waves), spanning modules on work, government, social networks, religion, inequality, the environment, health, family, national identity, and digital societies (767,484 responses from 14,356 respondents across \(616\) questions). The second is the Twin-2K-500 panel [@toubia2025database], covering psychological, economic, and behavioral items (1,127,649 responses from 2,058 participants across \(974\) questions). Combined, the corpus contains 1,895,133 responses from 16,414 respondents across 1,590 questions (\(761\) Likert score items and \(829\) categorical choice items). We partition the corpus strictly by question into 1,572 training items (1,881,950 observations) and \(18\) unseen validation items (13,183 observations across \(4\) US ISSP and \(14\) Twin-2K-500 questions).

Harmonized persona attributes. Respondents are represented by up to \(17\) harmonized demographic and attitudinal attributes (@apptbl-attributes). Eleven attributes appear in both surveys (sex, age, education, religion, religious attendance, political ideology, marital status, main activity, political party, household income, and US census region), four appear only in US ISSP (prior voting, urban-rural residence, union membership, and ancestry), and two appear only in Twin-2K-500 (race and household size). The 16,414 respondents span 16,338 distinct full-persona configurations, and unrecorded attributes for any respondent are omitted from the state prompt.

Decision models and optimization. We evaluate the Kev decision-model family [@palmer2026kev; @gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale] across three scales (Kev 0.8B, Kev 4B, and Kev 9B), comparing each off-the-shelf base checkpoint against its fine-tuned FT counterpart. Each base model is trained in bfloat16 precision using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, rank \(r = 64\), \(\alpha = 128\)) across all linear backbone projections alongside the trainable decision head. We optimize Equation 4 using AdamW (peak learning rate \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), weight decay \(0.01\), a \(10\%\) cosine warmup with cosine decay, and gradient clipping at \(1.0\)). Throughout training and held-out evaluation, validation cross-entropy is computed against observed individual one-hot responses \(\mathbf{y}_{i,q}\) under respondent \(i\)’s full persona \(\mathbf{s}_i\), and the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint is selected. At evaluation, off-the-shelf models use their released calibration temperatures (\(T = 2.35\) for 0.8B, \(T = 2.41\) for 4B, and \(T = 2.19\) for 9B), whereas all fine-tuned models use unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)) without post-hoc tuning.

Note. Training and validation splits partition the harmonized US ISSP (10 waves, 2015 to 2024) and Twin-2K-500 [@toubia2025database] microdata strictly by survey question. Held-out study rows report full / evaluation subsample counts for Study 1 [@kreps2020factors; @wang2026large] and Study 2 [@grummon2025nutrition].

Held-Out Survey Validation

Off-the-shelf decision models exhibit high cross-entropy on the \(18\) unseen validation questions (2.4262 bits for Kev 0.8B, 2.3586 bits for Kev 4B, and 2.3983 bits for Kev 9B), with little benefit from scale alone (Table 2 and Panel A of Figure 1). Distributional fine-tuning reduces held-out validation cross-entropy by \(26.0\%\) to \(27.5\%\), reaching 1.7963 bits on Kev 0.8B (\(0.34\) epochs), 1.7431 bits on Kev 4B (\(0.67\) epochs), and 1.7377 bits on Kev 9B (\(0.60\) epochs). Most of the reduction occurs within the first \(0.10\) epochs as the decision head adapts to subjective response distributions, followed by steady gains from persona conditioning.

To verify that survey training generalizes beyond survey phrasing, we also track zero-shot cross-entropy across all 33,798 choices of the held-out nutrition labeling trial [@grummon2025nutrition] (Section 5, Table 2, and Panel B of Figure 1). Whereas off-the-shelf models scale non-monotonically (2.0224, 2.1745, and 1.8494 bits), fine-tuning establishes a clear scaling hierarchy on out-of-sample choices (1.6686 bits on Kev 0.8B, 1.4899 bits on Kev 4B, and 1.3710 bits on Kev 9B).

Note. Cross-entropy (CE) is reported in bits (\(\log_2\)). Held-out survey validation spans 13,183 observations across 18 unseen US ISSP and Twin-2K-500 items; held-out RCT evaluation spans all 33,798 choices (N = 5,633 shoppers across 36 tasks) in @grummon2025nutrition. Off-the-shelf (Base) models use released calibration temperatures (\(T = 2.35\), \(2.41\), and \(2.19\)); fine-tuned (FT) models use unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)) at the lowest-validation-CE checkpoint (Kev 4B held-out RCT CE is evaluated at Epoch 1 completion because RCT tracking ran every 1,000 steps).

Note. Panel A tracks cross-entropy (in bits) across Epoch 1 on the held-out survey validation split (18 unseen US ISSP and Twin-2K-500 questions, n = 13,183; evaluated every 50 steps on 0.8B and 9B and every 200 steps on 4B), where circles denote the selected best validation checkpoint for each scale. Panel B tracks choice cross-entropy on the held-out front-of-package nutrition labeling trial [@grummon2025nutrition] (36 tasks, n = 33,798).

Study 1: Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis

To test whether fine-tuning on human response distributions generalizes to out-of-sample human studies, we estimate each held-out study’s econometric model on the predicted responses and compare the resulting parameters against the human estimates.

Experimental Design and Multinomial Logit Specification

Our first held-out study evaluates part-worth utility recovery on the COVID-19 vaccine conjoint dataset of @kreps2020factors, following the benchmark protocol of @wang2026large (Table 1). In this study, 1,971 US respondents each complete \(t \in \{1, \dots, 5\}\) paired choice tasks (9,855 observations). Each task presents two hypothetical vaccine profiles (Option A and Option B) with binary attribute vectors \(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}, \mathbf{x}_{i,t,B} \in \{0, 1\}^d\), alongside a no-vaccine outside option (Option C) whose utility is normalized to zero. Profiles vary across \(7\) attributes represented by \(d = 11\) non-reference binary indicators: efficacy (\(70\%\) and \(90\%\) vs. \(50\%\)), protection duration (\(5\) years vs. \(1\) year), major side effects (\(1\) in 1,000,000 vs. \(1\) in 10,000), minor side effects (\(1\) in \(30\) vs. \(1\) in \(10\)), regulatory status (emergency use authorization vs. full FDA approval), origin (China and the United Kingdom vs. the United States), and endorsement (Joe Biden, the US CDC, and the WHO vs. Donald Trump).

Under the standard Multinomial Logit (MNL) model [@wang2026large], choice probabilities for parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are: \[ P_m(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,m}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}, \qquad m \in \{A, B\}, \tag{5}\] with \(P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) - P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta})\). Given observed or predicted choice shares \((p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})\) across \(n\) choice sets, we estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) via Newton-CG maximum likelihood: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d} -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{(i,t)} \sum_{m \in \{A, B, C\}} p_{i,t,m} \log P_m(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{6}\] Human respondents and generative LLMs supply one-hot choices, whereas decision models supply continuous probabilities from Equation 2. Let \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* \in \mathbb{R}^{11}\) denote the ground-truth MNL vector estimated on all 9,855 human choices. Following @wang2026large, we evaluate models on a 200-respondent subsample (n = 1,000 choice sets) using Mean Absolute Percentage Error (\(\mathrm{MAPE}\)) and Mean Squared Error (\(\mathrm{MSE}\)): \[ \mathrm{MAPE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{|\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j|}{|\beta^*_j|} \times 100\%, \tag{7}\] \[ \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} (\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j)^2. \tag{8}\]

We test both unconditioned random personas and \(9\)-attribute respondent personas (US region, sex, age, education, race, income, religion, political party, and ideology; @apptbl-attributes). Decision models score the verbatim survey instrument [@kreps2020factors], whereas generative LLMs (Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at minimal and high thinking; Gemini 4 Argon at low and high thinking) use the @wang2026large prompt format (which additionally states country). Unlike @wang2026large, who excluded vaccine opt-out respondents due to LLM safety refusals, we retain the full untruncated respondent population and benchmark against MNL estimated directly on the 200 sampled humans.

Conjoint Estimation Results

Generative LLM bias and inverse scaling with reasoning. Estimating MNL on the 200 sampled humans yields the finite-sample benchmark of \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.1\%\) and \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0065\) (Table 3; @appfig-wang-random and @appfig-wang-persona). Consistent with @wang2026large, generative LLMs exhibit severe bias under random personas (\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 531.7\%\) to \(1507.7\%\); \(\mathrm{MSE} = 3.2073\) to \(26.2179\)). Conditioning on the \(9\) respondent attributes cuts generative LLM MSE by \(81.5\%\) to \(92.5\%\) (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.5925\) to \(2.1687\)), confirming that persona conditioning reduces aggregation error, yet MSE remains \(91\) to \(334\) times larger than the human subsample. Moreover, higher reasoning budgets and model scale worsen recovery: raising Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite thinking from minimal to high increases MSE from \(3.2073\) to \(14.4555\) (random) and from \(0.5925\) to \(1.1984\) (respondent), while Gemini 4 Argon degrades further to \(26.2179\) and \(2.1687\).

Outside-option collapse versus continuous decision readouts. Attribute-level estimates (Figure 2, Panel B; @apptbl-wang-coefs) isolate why generative LLMs fail. Whereas humans select the no-vaccine option (Option C) in \(21.0\%\) of full-sample tasks (\(20.3\%\) in the subsample), random-persona generative LLMs rarely opt out (\(3.0\%\) for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (minimal), \(0.3\%\) for Gemini 4 Argon (low), and \(0.0\%\) at high thinking; under respondent personas, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite drops from \(21.9\%\) to \(4.8\%\) at high thinking). Combined with deterministic profile selection, this outside-option collapse inflates MNL log-odds by an order of magnitude (e.g., respondent-persona Efficacy: 90% reaches \(+1.846\) to \(+3.538\) vs. human \(+0.746\), and Origin: China reaches \(-1.908\) to \(-3.556\) vs. \(-0.570\)), while Gemini 4 Argon (high) refuses \(16\) of \(1,000\) random-persona tasks (n = 984). Replacing token generation with continuous probabilities from Kev 9B (base) (\(T = 2.19\)) eliminates refusals and lowers MSE by an order of magnitude to \(0.2449\) (random) and \(0.0815\) (respondent). Discretizing Kev 9B (base) into one-hot argmax choices raises respondent-persona MSE nearly \(23\)-fold (\(0.0815\) to \(1.8678\)), confirming the value of continuous probabilities in Equation 6.

Gains from distributional survey fine-tuning. Without using any target-study conjoint data, Kev 9B (FT) (\(T = 1.00\)) cuts MNL MSE relative to Kev 9B (base) by \(64.3\%\) under random personas (\(0.2449\) to \(0.0875\); \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 84.0\%\)) and by \(67.2\%\) under respondent personas (\(0.0815\) to \(0.0267\); \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 56.4\%\); Table 3). Fine-tuning also lowers choice cross-entropy (1.6370 to 1.5192 bits random; 1.5255 to 1.4449 bits respondent) and restores realistic opt-out shares (\(19.0\%\) and \(28.1\%\) vs. \(11.9\%\) and \(16.4\%\) for base). In Figure 2 (Panel A; @apptbl-wang-coefs), Kev 9B (base) overstates efficacy and institutional endorsements, placing only \(5\) of \(11\) estimates inside the human subsample’s \(95\%\) confidence intervals. Kev 9B (FT) places \(8\) of \(11\) estimates inside the subsample \(95\%\) intervals, closely matching humans on efficacy (\(+0.217\) and \(+0.562\) vs. \(+0.361\) and \(+0.746\)), EUA (\(-0.123\) vs. \(-0.115\)), origin (\(-0.192\) and \(-0.553\) vs. \(-0.140\) and \(-0.570\)), and endorsements (Biden \(+0.135\) vs. \(+0.095\); WHO \(+0.516\) vs. \(+0.273\); US CDC \(+0.621\) vs. \(+0.416\), down from base \(+1.017\)), while conservatively attenuating Duration: 5 years (\(+0.035\) vs. \(+0.261\)) and Major side effects (\(+0.043\) vs. \(+0.304\)).

Study 2: Front-of-Package Nutrition Labeling Trial

Experimental Design and OLS Specification

Our second held-out study evaluates causal treatment-effect recovery in the front-of-package (FOP) nutrition labeling randomized clinical trial of @grummon2025nutrition (Table 1). In this trial, N = 5,633 US grocery shoppers are randomized to one of six between-subjects labeling arms \(a_i\): Positive (reference arm), Nutrition Info, High In warning, Positive + Nutrition Info, Positive + High In, or Spectrum (1-to-5 star rating). Each shopper completes \(q \in \{1, \dots, 6\}\) three-option product healthfulness choice tasks across distinct food and beverage categories (36 arm-by-category tasks, 33,798 choices), where option \(y_1\) denotes the objectively healthier product.

For each shopper \(i\), we compute the mean probability of selecting the healthier product across the six tasks, \(\bar{p}_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{q=1}^6 p_{i,q,1}\), where \(p_{i,q,1}\) is the binary choice indicator \(y_{i,q,1}\) for human shoppers and the predicted probability \(P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_1 \mid \mathbf{s}_i; \theta)\) under arm \(a_i\) for decision models. Following @grummon2025nutrition, we regress \(\bar{p}_i\) on the five treatment-arm indicators via ordinary least squares (OLS) with HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors to obtain \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^5\) relative to Positive. We evaluate models on a 200-shopper subsample (n = 1,200 choices) against the full-sample human vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) (N = 5,633) using \(\mathrm{MAPE}\), \(\mathrm{MSE}\), and Spearman rank correlation \(\rho\). Each shopper profile contains \(13\) attributes (@apptbl-attributes): \(6\) demographic attributes seen in survey training (sex, age, education, income, race, and household size) and \(7\) covariates completely unseen during training (Hispanic ethnicity, number of children, SNAP receipt, WIC receipt, Nutrition Facts label usage, online grocery share, and nutrition literacy score).

Trial Estimation and Attribute Generalization Results

Calibration and treatment-effect recovery across model scales. Estimating OLS on the 200 sampled shoppers yields the finite-sample benchmark of \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.7\%\) and \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0016\) (\(\rho = +0.40\), as finite-sample noise swaps the nearly tied Nutrition Info [\(+0.184\)] and Positive + Nutrition Info [\(+0.179\)] arms; Table 4; @appfig-grummon2025-random and @appfig-grummon2025-persona). Under \(13\)-attribute personas, off-the-shelf base models exhibit inverted scaling in choice cross-entropy (1.4770, 1.5124, and 1.5405 bits across 0.8B, 4B, and 9B) and higher OLS MSE than under random prompts (\(0.0074\) vs. \(0.0050\) on 0.8B, \(0.0088\) vs. \(0.0028\) on 4B, and \(0.0047\) vs. \(0.0034\) on 9B). Survey fine-tuning (\(T = 1.00\)) establishes monotonic scaling in choice cross-entropy (1.5536, 1.4659, and 1.3479 bits). On OLS recovery, Kev 0.8B (FT) improves rank correlation to \(\rho = +0.70\) under both persona regimes, and Kev 4B (FT) lowers respondent-persona MAPE from \(95.9\%\) to \(83.0\%\) (\(\rho = +0.80\); \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0099\)). At the \(9\text{B}\) scale, Kev 9B (FT) reaches \(\rho = +0.90\), lowers respondent-persona MAPE from \(63.7\%\) to \(44.3\%\), and cuts OLS MSE by \(53.2\%\) (\(0.0047\) to \(0.0022\); and by \(44.1\%\) under random prompts, \(0.0034\) to \(0.0019\)), approaching the 200-shopper human subsample (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0016\)).

Interpretive labels versus visual warning shields. Arm-level estimates (Figure 3, Panel A; @apptbl-grummon2025-coefs) clarify both the accuracy and modality limits of text-only silicon sampling. Kev 9B (base) underpredicts the "They're the same" tie option (\(26.7\%\) vs. human \(31.6\%\) in Positive), favors the less healthy item in both High In arms, and overshoots both Nutrition Info arms (\(+0.224\) and \(+0.229\)). Kev 9B (FT) restores realistic tie shares (\(34.9\%\) in Positive), places all five arm estimates inside the 200-shopper subsample’s \(95\%\) confidence intervals, and closely matches \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) on all three symmetrical interpretive arms: Nutrition Info (\(+0.177\) vs. \(+0.184\)), Positive + Nutrition Info (\(+0.168\) vs. \(+0.179\)), and Spectrum (\(+0.139\) vs. \(+0.108\)). Remaining error concentrates in the two asymmetric High In arms (High In: \(-0.010\) vs. \(+0.054\), up from base \(-0.047\); Positive + High In: \(+0.042\) vs. \(+0.119\), up from base \(+0.023\)), where graphical black exclamation-mark shields in @grummon2025nutrition were serialized into text (Front-of-package label: None vs. High In: ... | FDA.gov), creating institutional-tag salience ambiguity without visual warning cues.

Generalization to unseen shopper attributes. To test whether fine-tuning memorizes training demographics or generalizes to novel covariates, we evaluate single-attribute conditioning across the \(6\) seen and \(7\) unseen shopper attributes (Figure 3, Panel B; @apptbl-grummon2025-personas) as well as separate \(6\)-attribute seen and \(7\)-attribute unseen bundles. Kev 9B (base) degrades on unseen covariates (1.5328 bits unseen mean vs. 1.4866 bits seen; bundles 1.5316 vs. 1.4243 bits), performing worse than uniform guessing (1.5850 bits) on Nutrition Facts label usage (1.6661) and Share of groceries bought online (1.6016). Kev 9B (FT) lowers cross-entropy on all \(13\) attributes and achieves a larger reduction on the \(7\) unseen attributes (1.5328 to 1.3473 bits, \(-12.1\%\); bundle 1.5316 to 1.3493 bits, \(-11.9\%\)) than on the \(6\) seen attributes (1.4866 to 1.3477 bits, \(-9.3\%\); bundle 1.4243 to 1.3465 bits, \(-5.5\%\)). This compresses the seen-versus-unseen calibration gap from 0.0462 to 0.0004 bits across single attributes (0.1073 to 0.0028 bits across bundles).
