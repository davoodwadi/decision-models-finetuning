Training decision models from choice microdata and published regression tables for silicon sampling

Authors
Affiliation

Davood Wadi

McGill University

Yu Ma

McGill University

Abstract
Using off-the-shelf generative large language models (LLMs) to simulate human respondents (silicon sampling) severely biases downstream econometric inference, while standard fine-tuning requires individual choice microdata that most published studies never release. We align single-pass decision models that output normalized choice probabilities directly over valid options without text generation, making linear regression estimators directly differentiable in model weights. To unlock studies that report only experimental designs and aggregate regression tables, we derive an unbiased mini-batch population-\(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) loss that pairs a frozen base-probability control variate with split-sample cross-products, stabilized by a Kullback-Leibler anchor on table-only data or respondent-level cross-entropy (CE) on available microdata. We evaluate open-weight decision models across five data-access regimes trained on consumer delivery and electricity-plan choice studies and tested zero-shot on an airline conjoint experiment and a six-arm nutrition-labeling randomized trial. Microdata CE fine-tuning substantially lowers out-of-sample treatment-effect mean squared error (MSE), table-only training beats the off-the-shelf baseline without a single respondent choice, and hybrid microdata-plus-table training transfers missing price and green-energy trade-offs to close [TBD]% to [TBD]% of the gap toward a full-microdata oracle.

Keywords. Decision models, Silicon sampling, Econometric inference, Conjoint analysis, Marketing research

Introduction

Simulating consumer choices, attitudes, and survey responses with foundation models, commonly referred to as silicon sampling, has attracted growing interest in marketing and behavioral research. In marketing research, the primary goal is downstream econometric inference, such as estimating attribute slopes in conjoint analysis or causal treatment effects in randomized experiments. Currently, research relies on off-the-shelf generative large language models (LLMs) that generate text autoregressively. However, replacing human respondents with off-the-shelf generative LLM outputs severely biases downstream regressions because post-training for deterministic reasoning and safety compliance collapses choice uncertainty and triggers safety refusals on sensitive options (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026). Whereas existing post-inference methods adjust or reweight fixed model outputs using target-study human data (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), we align models before inference through their output architecture and training supervision.

Architecturally, we adopt single-pass decision models (Palmer 2026). Instead of generating text tokens, a decision model takes a respondent persona and a structured choice task and outputs a normalized probability distribution over the valid options in one forward pass. This readout eliminates sampling variance and safety refusals while making linear regression estimators, such as ordinary least squares (OLS), directly differentiable in the model weights. Yet architecture alone is insufficient because off-the-shelf decision models are pretrained on single-label objective benchmarks, treating disagreement among respondents who share a persona as classification error and biasing regression estimates. Minimizing cross-entropy (CE) on individual choice microdata resolves this miscalibration when respondent choices are observed. In practice, however, raw choice microdata is scarce, proprietary, or privacy-restricted, whereas thousands of published marketing studies release only experimental designs and aggregate regression tables.

Training a decision model directly on published regression coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) via mini-batch gradient descent presents two statistical challenges. First, population OLS slopes depend on choice probabilities across all tasks in a study rather than one mini-batch; fitting OLS within small batches chases finite-sample noise, whereas squaring a single-batch estimate of the population slopes introduces variance bias that penalizes persona heterogeneity. Second, matching a small vector of aggregate slopes leaves individual choice entropy unconstrained, allowing predicted probabilities to drift toward zero or one. We address the first challenge with an unbiased population-\(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) loss that uses frozen base-model probabilities as a control variate and multiplies estimation errors across two disjoint half-batches without using respondent choices. We address the second challenge by pairing this loss with an entropy anchor, using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence toward base probabilities for table-only training or respondent-level CE on available microdata for hybrid training.

We evaluate this framework across a five-regime data-access ladder using open-weight decision models (Palmer 2026) trained on a US drone-delivery discrete-choice experiment (Berke et al. 2023) and an electricity-plan conjoint study (Wu 2025), and evaluated zero-shot on an unseen sustainable-aviation-fuel flight conjoint (Goldfarb et al. 2026) and a six-arm nutrition-labeling randomized trial (Grummon et al. 2025). Our design establishes three contributions. First, CE fine-tuning on respondent microdata substantially lowers zero-shot treatment-effect mean squared error (MSE) on unseen experiments relative to the off-the-shelf model, whether training on single-study delivery microdata or on full microdata across both studies as an upper-bound oracle. Second, without access to respondent choices, training solely on the eight published electricity slopes of Wu (2025) improves out-of-sample MSE over the off-the-shelf baseline, and combining those published slopes with Berke et al. (2023) delivery microdata transfers household price and green-energy trade-offs that delivery microdata alone lacks. Third, progressing from table-only to hybrid training closes [TBD]% to [TBD]% of the out-of-sample MSE gap toward the unattainable full-microdata oracle.

Literature review

Early work in silicon sampling prompted generative LLMs with demographic profiles to simulate human responses in political, economic, and marketing surveys (Argyle et al. 2023, Brand et al. 2023, Horton et al. 2023, Santurkar et al. 2023, Durmus et al. 2024, Goli and Singh 2024, Sarstedt et al. 2024). Although generative LLMs often capture the direction of average preferences, prior studies document systematic discrepancies from human populations, including mode collapse onto a single option, attenuated within-group variance, demographic stereotyping, and prompt sensitivity (Santurkar et al. 2023, Koo et al. 2024, Shu et al. 2024, Wadi and Fredette 2025, Deas and McKeown 2025, Kabir et al. 2025, Zahradnickova et al. 2026, Jung et al. 2026). In marketing applications, these distortions severely bias downstream econometric estimation (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026). In choice-based conjoint analysis, relying on simulated choices or naively pooling them with human data biases preference estimates, while scaling model capability or chain-of-thought reasoning worsens error by inducing overly deterministic choices (Wang et al. 2026). Recent marketing studies address these discrepancies after inference by treating the language model as a fixed generator, using target-study human benchmark data to statistically debias downstream regression parameters (Wang et al. 2026, Angelopoulos et al. 2023; AAE) or optimally reweight ensembles of candidate prompts, personas, and model checkpoints (Leng et al. 2026; HuMCal). Complementing these post-inference methods, we align predictions inside the model before inference through both output architecture and training supervision.

Architecturally, several studies avoid the cost and sampling variance of repeated Monte Carlo text generation by extracting next-token probabilities over option symbols (e.g., A, B, and C) from causal LLMs (Kadavath et al. 2022, Kuribayashi et al. 2024, Bentegeac et al. 2025, Wadi et al. 2026). Yet symbol-level readouts remain sensitive to vocabulary and token-frequency priors. Decision models, also known as System One architectures, resolve this sensitivity via a single-pass scoring head (Asseri and Asseri 2026, Deußer et al. 2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Li 2026, Li and Wagle 2026, Palmer 2026). This head evaluates each supplied option directly in representation space and restricts probability mass strictly to the valid options without open-ended generation or safety refusals. Crucially for econometric training, outputting exact choice probabilities in a single forward pass makes linear regression operators over experimental designs directly differentiable with respect to model parameters. However, existing decision models are pretrained on single-label objective benchmarks such as mathematics, code verification, and factual question answering, causing severe probability miscalibration on subjective choice tasks (Gao et al. 2026).

On the training dimension, existing fine-tuning approaches for silicon sampling require access to unreleased response data. At the token level, Wang et al. (2026) fine-tuned GPT-4o on 50 target-study conjoint tasks under a generic persona to emit a single choice letter, finding that small-sample single-token supervision worsened standalone parameter error relative to the human benchmark. Recent studies improve calibration by supervising causal language models or decision heads on large-scale survey microdata and subgroup response histograms (Suh et al. 2025, Ku et al. 2026, Huang et al. 2026). When respondent-level choice microdata is available, minimizing CE on a probabilistic decision head effectively calibrates conditional choice probabilities across personas. Yet across conjoint analysis, discrete-choice modeling (McFadden 1972, Train 2009), and randomized online or field trials, published articles and archival industry reports rarely disclose raw respondent choices, reporting instead experimental designs and estimated regression tables. We bridge this data gap by supervising decision models directly on published population OLS coefficients via an unbiased split-sample moment loss, deployed either standalone with a base-distribution KL anchor when no microdata exists or jointly alongside respondent-level CE when augmenting in-house microdata with external literature tables.

Methodology

Decision model architecture and choice probabilities. Consider an experimental or survey study consisting of \(N\) discrete-choice tasks indexed by \(t \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Each task \(t\) pairs a respondent’s multi-attribute persona state \(\mathbf{s}_t\) (e.g., sex, age, education, income, political party, and race) with a question stem presenting \(K_t\) discrete answer options \(\{1, \dots, K_t\}\). When individual choice microdata is available, let \(\mathbf{y}_t = (y_{t,1}, \dots, y_{t,K_t})^\top \in \{0, 1\}^{K_t}\) denote the observed one-hot choice vector on task \(t\), where \(y_{t,k} = 1\) if the respondent selected option \(k\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Given persona state \(\mathbf{s}_t\), a single-pass decision model (Palmer 2026) scores all \(K_t\) options in one causal forward pass without a vocabulary generation head. The prompt places \(\mathbf{s}_t\) in a state segment, followed by the question stem, each option \(k\) enclosed in dedicated option delimiters, and a terminal decision token appended after the final option. Because the terminal decision token follows all \(K_t\) options, its final-layer hidden state \(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_t(\mathbf{s}_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) causally attends to the persona, the question stem, and the full option set. Let \(\mathbf{h}_{t,k}(\mathbf{s}_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) denote the final-layer hidden state at the closing delimiter of option \(k\). Using learned affine projections \((\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{b}_q)\) and \((\mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{b}_k)\) with \(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}} \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) and \(\mathbf{b}_q, \mathbf{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}}}\), the decision head computes a scalar score \(z_{t,k}(\mathbf{s}_t; \theta)\) for each option \(k\): \[ z_{t,k}(\mathbf{s}_t; \theta) = \frac{\big(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_t(\mathbf{s}_t) + \mathbf{b}_q\big)^\top \big(\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{t,k}(\mathbf{s}_t) + \mathbf{b}_k\big)}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}. \tag{1}\] Applying a softmax function with readout temperature \(T > 0\) yields the normalized choice probability vector \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) = (p_{t,1}(\theta), \dots, p_{t,K_t}(\theta))^\top\) in a single pass: \[ p_{t,k}(\theta) = P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_t = k \mid \mathbf{s}_t; \theta) = \frac{\exp\!\big(z_{t,k}(\mathbf{s}_t; \theta) / T\big)}{\sum_{m=1}^{K_t} \exp\!\big(z_{t,m}(\mathbf{s}_t; \theta) / T\big)}. \tag{2}\] When respondent choices \(\mathbf{y}_t\) are observed, model parameters \(\theta\) can be supervised directly via mini-batch cross-entropy over a sampled task batch \(S \subset \{1, \dots, N\}\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta; S) = -\frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{t \in S} \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} y_{t,k} \log p_{t,k}(\theta). \tag{3}\]

Population OLS operators and published targets. Concatenating option probabilities across all \(N\) tasks in a study yields the flat vector \(\mathbf{p}(\theta) = (\mathbf{p}_1(\theta)^\top, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N(\theta)^\top)^\top \in [0, 1]^M\), where \(M = \sum_{t=1}^N K_t\), with corresponding stacked human choices \(\mathbf{y} \in \{0, 1\}^M\). Any linear econometric specification estimated on the study can be written over \(R\) regression rows via a full-column-rank design matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{R \times c}\) (comprising an intercept, any nuisance controls, and \(d\) focal treatment columns indexed by \(\mathcal{T} \subset \{1, \dots, c\}\)) and a fixed sparse weight matrix \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{R \times M}\) that maps option probabilities to regression outcomes \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{p}(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^R\) (e.g., selecting each displayed profile’s choice probability in conjoint average marginal component effects or the healthier item’s choice probability in a randomized trial). Dividing by the human outcome standard deviation \(\sigma > 0\) places coefficients across studies on a common dimensionless scale. Using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse \(\mathbf{X}^\dagger = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\), define the fixed standardized population slope operator: \[ \mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{\sigma} \big(\mathbf{X}^\dagger\big)_{\mathcal{T}, :} \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times M}, \tag{4}\] partitioned by task into column blocks \(\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{B}_1, \dots, \mathbf{B}_N]\) with \(\mathbf{B}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times K_t}\). Under Equation 4, the model’s standardized population OLS slopes are an exact linear map of its single-pass probabilities, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\theta) = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^N \mathbf{B}_t \mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\), and a published regression table supplies the standardized human target \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (which equals \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\) without requiring individual choices \(\mathbf{y}\)). When a study estimates \(G > 1\) regressions, we construct \((\mathbf{B}^{(g)}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^{*(g)})\) for each \(g \in \{1, \dots, G\}\) and average the resulting regression losses equally across \(g\).

Unbiased split-sample population-beta loss. To align model-implied slopes \(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\theta)\) with a published table \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\), our target population objective is the standardized slope MSE: \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{d} \left\lVert \mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}(\theta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}^* \right\rVert_2^2. \tag{5}\] Evaluating Equation 5 directly per optimization step is intractable because \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}(\theta)\) sums over all \(N\) tasks whereas stochastic gradient descent evaluates a mini-batch \(S\) of \(|S| \ll N\) tasks. Naive mini-batch surrogates fail for two reasons. Refitting OLS inside a small batch \(S\) requires unreleased choices \(\mathbf{y}_t\) and squares ill-conditioned small-sample design inverses, whereas substituting a single-batch estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_S(\theta)\) into \(\frac{1}{d}\lVert \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_S(\theta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}^* \rVert_2^2\) adds positive variance bias \(\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_S(\theta))\), which increases with across-persona heterogeneity in \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta)\) and penalizes the model for conditioning on respondent personas.

To construct an unbiased, low-variance gradient estimator without respondent choices or stale cached predictions, we run a single no-gradient evaluation pass at initialization \(\theta_0\) to record frozen base probabilities \(\mathbf{p}_0 = \mathbf{p}(\theta_0)\) and base slopes \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}_0\). Rewriting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\theta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}_0 + \sum_{t=1}^N \mathbf{B}_t (\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) - \mathbf{p}_{0,t})\), any uniform subsample \(S_m \subset \{1, \dots, N\}\) yields an exact control-variate estimator of the current population slopes: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_m(\theta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}_0 + \frac{N}{|S_m|} \sum_{t \in S_m} \mathbf{B}_t \big(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) - \mathbf{p}_{0,t}\big). \tag{6}\] Because \(\mathbf{p}_0\) is fixed, \(\mathbf{p}_{0,t}\) cancels in expectation at every step so \(\mathbb{E}_{S_m}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_m(\theta)] = \boldsymbol{\beta}(\theta)\) without staleness bias, while subtracting \(\mathbf{p}_{0,t}\) reduces mini-batch variance. Partitioning each shuffled batch \(S\) (\(|S| \ge 2\)) into two disjoint halves \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) of sizes \(\lfloor |S| / 2 \rfloor\) and \(\lceil |S| / 2 \rceil\), we define the split-sample cross loss as the inner product of their estimation errors: \[ \mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}(\theta; S_1, S_2) = \frac{1}{d} \big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1(\theta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}^*\big)^\top \big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_2(\theta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}^*\big). \tag{7}\] Up to negligible \(\mathcal{O}(1/N)\) finite-population sampling covariance without replacement, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1(\theta)\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_2(\theta)\) are independent unbiased estimators of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\theta)\), implying \(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}(\theta; S_1, S_2)] = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}(\theta; S_1, S_2)] = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}(\theta)\) (individual step realizations of Equation 7 may be negative).

Entropy anchoring across training regimes. Because \(d \ll M\), minimizing Equation 7 alone constrains only \(d\) linear projections of \(\mathbf{p}(\theta)\) and leaves the \((M - d)\)-dimensional nullspace \(\ker(\mathbf{B})\) unconstrained. Without an entropy anchor, prolonged optimization can sharpen logits along unconstrained directions, driving task probabilities toward zero or one while maintaining \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}(\theta) \approx \boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) and inflating out-of-sample treatment-effect magnitudes. In the table-only regime, where no microdata is available for any study, we regularize predicted probabilities toward the frozen base distribution \(\mathbf{p}_{0,t}\) via a forward KL penalty with weight \(\alpha > 0\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{table}}(\theta; S) = \mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}(\theta; S_1, S_2) + \alpha \, \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{t \in S} \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} p_{0,t,k} \log \frac{p_{0,t,k}}{p_{t,k}(\theta)}. \tag{8}\] Evaluating the KL anchor requires zero additional forward passes because \(\mathbf{p}_{0,t}\) is already stored in memory, and its logit gradient \(\mathbf{p}_t(\theta) - \mathbf{p}_{0,t}\) prevents probabilities from collapsing onto zero or one. In the hybrid regime, where in-house choice microdata \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{micro}}\) is paired with external table-only studies \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{table}}\), we interleave single-study mini-batches and jointly minimize respondent-level CE on \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{micro}}\) alongside weighted population-\(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) supervision (\(\lambda > 0\)) on \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{table}}\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hybrid}}(\theta; S) = \mathbb{I}(S \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{micro}}) \, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta; S) + \mathbb{I}(S \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{table}}) \, \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}(\theta; S_1, S_2). \tag{9}\] Under Equation 9, \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\) calibrates global logit scale and choice uncertainty to empirical human randomness, while \(\mathcal{L}_{\beta\text{-cross}}\) transfers missing attribute trade-offs from external regression tables.

Survey Training and Validation

Survey microdata and question split. We construct a harmonized training corpus from two public US survey microdata sources (Table 1). The first comprises \(10\) consecutive US waves of the International Social Survey Programme (US ISSP, 2015 to 2024; Table W1), spanning modules on work, government, social networks, religion, inequality, the environment, health, family, national identity, and digital societies (767,484 responses from 14,356 respondents across \(616\) questions). The second is the Twin-2K-500 panel (Toubia et al. 2025), covering psychological, economic, and behavioral items (1,127,649 responses from 2,058 participants across \(974\) questions). Combined, the corpus contains 1,895,133 responses from 16,414 respondents across 1,590 questions (\(761\) Likert score items and \(829\) categorical choice items). We partition the corpus strictly by question into 1,572 training items (1,881,950 observations) and \(18\) unseen validation items (13,183 observations across \(4\) US ISSP and \(14\) Twin-2K-500 questions).

Harmonized persona attributes. Respondents are represented by up to \(17\) harmonized demographic and attitudinal attributes (Table W2). Eleven attributes appear in both surveys (sex, age, education, religion, religious attendance, political ideology, marital status, main activity, political party, household income, and US census region), four appear only in US ISSP (prior voting, urban-rural residence, union membership, and ancestry), and two appear only in Twin-2K-500 (race and household size). The 16,414 respondents span 16,338 distinct full-persona configurations, and unrecorded attributes for any respondent are omitted from the state prompt.

Decision models and optimization. We evaluate the Kev decision-model family (Gao et al. 2026, Palmer 2026) across three scales (Kev 0.8B, Kev 4B, and Kev 9B), comparing each off-the-shelf base checkpoint against its fine-tuned FT counterpart. Each base model is trained in bfloat16 precision using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, rank \(r = 64\), \(\alpha = 128\)) across all linear backbone projections alongside the trainable decision head. We optimize Equation 3 using AdamW (peak learning rate \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), weight decay \(0.01\), a \(10\%\) cosine warmup with cosine decay, and gradient clipping at \(1.0\)). Throughout training and held-out evaluation, validation cross-entropy is computed against observed individual one-hot responses \(\mathbf{y}_{i,q}\) under respondent \(i\)’s full persona \(\mathbf{s}_i\), and the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint is selected. At evaluation, off-the-shelf models use their released calibration temperatures (\(T = 2.35\) for 0.8B, \(T = 2.41\) for 4B, and \(T = 2.19\) for 9B), whereas all fine-tuned models use unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)) without post-hoc tuning.

Note. Training and validation splits partition the harmonized US ISSP (10 waves, 2015 to 2024) and Twin-2K-500 (Toubia et al. 2025) microdata strictly by survey question. Held-out study rows report full / evaluation subsample counts for Study 1 (Kreps et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2026) and Study 2 (Grummon et al. 2025).

Held-Out Survey Validation

Off-the-shelf decision models exhibit high cross-entropy on the \(18\) unseen validation questions, and larger models do not reliably perform better (2.4262 bits on Kev 0.8B, 2.3586 bits on Kev 4B, and 2.3983 bits on Kev 9B; Table 2 and Figure 1). Distributional fine-tuning reduces held-out validation cross-entropy by \(26.0\%\) to \(27.5\%\) and improves accuracy steadily with model scale, reaching 1.7963 bits on Kev 0.8B (\(0.34\) epochs), 1.7431 bits on Kev 4B (\(0.67\) epochs), and 1.7377 bits on Kev 9B (\(0.60\) epochs). Most of the reduction occurs within the first \(0.10\) epochs as the decision head adapts to subjective response distributions, followed by steady gains from persona conditioning.

Off-the-shelf (Base) models use released calibration temperatures (\(T = 2.35\), \(2.41\), and \(2.19\)); fine-tuned (FT) models use unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)) at the lowest-validation-CE checkpoint and at Epoch 1 completion.

Study 1: Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis

To test whether fine-tuning on human response distributions generalizes to out-of-sample human studies, we estimate each held-out study’s econometric model on the predicted responses and compare the resulting regression parameters against the human estimates.

Experimental Design and Multinomial Logit Specification

For our first held-out study, following the benchmark protocol of Wang et al. (2026), we use a COVID-19 vaccine conjoint dataset (Kreps et al. 2020), summarized in Table 1, to investigate part-worth utility recovery. In this study, 1,971 US respondents each complete \(t \in \{1, \dots, 5\}\) paired choice tasks (9,855 observations). Each task presents two hypothetical vaccine profiles (Option A and Option B) with binary attribute vectors \(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}, \mathbf{x}_{i,t,B} \in \{0, 1\}^d\), alongside a no-vaccine outside option (Option C) whose utility is normalized to zero. Profiles vary across \(7\) attributes coded as \(d = 11\) non-reference binary indicators (Table W3).

Under the standard Multinomial Logit (MNL) model (Wang et al. 2026), choice probabilities for parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are: \[ P_m(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,m}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}, \qquad m \in \{A, B\}, \tag{10}\] with \(P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) - P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta})\). Given observed or predicted choice shares \((p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})\) across \(n\) choice sets, we estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) via Newton-CG maximum likelihood: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d} -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{(i,t)} \sum_{m \in \{A, B, C\}} p_{i,t,m} \log P_m(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}). \tag{11}\] Human respondents and generative LLMs supply one-hot choices, whereas decision models supply continuous probabilities from Equation 2. Let \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* \in \mathbb{R}^{11}\) denote the ground-truth MNL vector estimated on all 9,855 human choices. Following Wang et al. (2026), we evaluate models on a 200-respondent subsample (n = 1,000 choice sets) using Mean Absolute Percentage Error (\(\mathrm{MAPE}\)) and Mean Squared Error (\(\mathrm{MSE}\)): \[ \mathrm{MAPE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{|\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j|}{|\beta^*_j|} \times 100\%, \tag{12}\] \[ \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} (\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j)^2. \tag{13}\]

We test both unconditioned random personas and \(9\)-attribute respondent personas (US region, gender, age, education, race, income, religion, political party, and ideology; Table W2). Decision models score the verbatim survey instrument (Kreps et al. 2020), whereas generative LLMs (Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at minimal and high thinking; Gemini 4 Argon at low and high thinking) use the Wang et al. (2026) prompt format (which additionally states country). Unlike Wang et al. (2026), who excluded vaccine opt-out respondents due to LLM safety refusals, we retain the full untruncated respondent population and benchmark against MNL estimated directly on the 200 sampled humans.

Conjoint Estimation Results

Generative LLM bias. Estimating MNL on the 200 sampled humans yields a finite-sample benchmark of \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0065\) (Table 3; Figure W1 and Figure W2). Consistent with Wang et al. (2026), generative LLMs exhibit severe bias under random personas (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 3.2073\) to \(26.2179\)). Conditioning on the \(9\) respondent attributes cuts error substantially (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.5925\) to \(2.1687\)), yet estimates remain far from the human benchmark. Moreover, higher reasoning budgets and model scale worsen parameter recovery. Under respondent personas, raising Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite thinking from minimal to high doubles MSE (\(0.5925\) to \(1.1984\)), while Gemini 4 Argon degrades further (\(1.7637\) at low and \(2.1687\) at high).

Why choice probabilities matter. Generative LLMs bias conjoint estimation because discrete text generation collapses choice uncertainty and triggers safety refusals. Under random personas, generative LLMs rarely select the no-vaccine option (\(0.0\%\) to \(3.0\%\) vs. \(20.3\%\) for humans), while Gemini 4 Argon (high) refuses \(16\) of \(1,000\) tasks. Even under respondent personas, near-deterministic profile selection pushes conditional choice shares toward zero or one, causing severe MNL scale inflation (Figure 2, Panel B; Table W3): Efficacy: 90% reaches \(+1.846\) to \(+3.538\) versus \(+0.746\) for humans. Scoring options directly via Kev 9B (base) (\(T = 2.19\)) eliminates refusals and cuts MSE by an order of magnitude (\(0.2449\) random; \(0.0815\) respondent). Collapsing those same Kev 9B (base) probabilities into one-hot top choices inflates respondent-persona MSE nearly \(23\)-fold (\(0.0815\) to \(1.8678\)), showing that continuous probability readouts–rather than discrete choices–are essential to prevent scale distortion.

Distributional survey fine-tuning. Evaluated zero-shot–without training or calibration on conjoint data–Kev 9B (FT) reduces MNL MSE relative to Kev 9B (base) by \(64.3\%\) under random personas (\(0.2449\) to \(0.0875\)) and by \(67.2\%\) under respondent personas (\(0.0815\) to \(0.0267\); Table 3). Fine-tuning also lowers choice cross-entropy (1.6370 to 1.5192 bits under random personas; 1.5255 to 1.4449 bits under respondent personas) and raises predicted no-vaccine shares (\(19.0\%\) and \(28.1\%\), up from \(11.9\%\) and \(16.4\%\) for Kev 9B (base)). At the attribute level (Figure 2, Panel A; Table W3), Kev 9B (base) overestimates the part-worths of vaccine efficacy and institutional endorsements, with only \(5\) of \(11\) estimates falling within the human subsample’s \(95\%\) confidence intervals. By contrast, Kev 9B (FT) recovers \(8\) of \(11\) estimates within those \(95\%\) confidence intervals, closely aligning with full-sample human part-worths on efficacy, FDA Emergency Use Authorization, country of origin, and endorsements while attenuating protection duration and rare major side effects toward zero.

Study 2: Front-of-Package Nutrition Labeling Trial

Experimental Design and OLS Specification

Our second held-out study evaluates causal treatment-effect recovery in a front-of-package nutrition labeling trial (Grummon et al. 2025). In this experiment, N = 5,633 US grocery shoppers are randomized to one of six between-subjects labeling arms (Positive serves as the reference arm; Table W4). Each shopper completes \(q \in \{1, \dots, 6\}\) three-option product choice tasks across food and beverage categories, where \(y_1\) denotes the objectively healthier option.

For each shopper \(i\), we compute the mean probability of selecting the healthier product across the six tasks, \(\bar{p}_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{q=1}^6 p_{i,q,1}\), where \(p_{i,q,1}\) is the binary choice indicator \(y_{i,q,1}\) for human shoppers and the predicted probability \(P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_1 \mid \mathbf{s}_i; \theta)\) under the assigned arm for decision models. Following Grummon et al. (2025), we regress \(\bar{p}_i\) on the five treatment-arm indicators via ordinary least squares (OLS) with HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors to obtain \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^5\) relative to Positive. We evaluate models on a 200-shopper subsample (n = 1,200 choices) against the full-sample human vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) (N = 5,633). Each shopper profile contains \(13\) attributes (Table W2): \(6\) demographic attributes seen in survey training (sex, age, education, income, race, and household size) and \(7\) covariates completely unseen during training (Hispanic ethnicity, number of children, SNAP receipt, WIC receipt, Nutrition Facts label usage, online grocery share, and nutrition literacy score).

Trial Estimation and Attribute Generalization Results

Treatment-effect recovery. Under \(13\)-attribute shopper personas, scaling off-the-shelf models from 0.8B to 9B worsens choice cross-entropy (1.4770 to 1.5405 bits; Table 4) and yields higher OLS MSE than unconditioned random prompts at every scale (Figure W3; Figure W4). Survey fine-tuning reverses this scaling pattern, steadily lowering cross-entropy from 1.5536 bits (0.8B) to 1.3479 bits (9B). Whereas smaller fine-tuned models (0.8B and 4B) improve arm rank ordering or MAPE without reducing OLS MSE, Kev 9B (FT) improves every metric. It reaches a Spearman rank correlation of \(\rho = +0.90\), lowers respondent-persona MAPE from \(63.7\%\) to \(44.3\%\), and reduces OLS MSE by \(53.2\%\) (\(0.0047\) to \(0.0022\); and by \(44.1\%\) to \(0.0019\) under random prompts), approaching the 200-shopper human benchmark (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0016\), \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.7\%\)). At the arm level (Figure 3, Panel A; Table W4), Kev 9B (FT) places all five estimates within the human subsample’s \(95\%\) confidence intervals, closely matching full-sample effects on the three symmetrical interpretive labels (Nutrition Info, Positive + Nutrition Info, and Spectrum). Remaining error concentrates in the two asymmetric High In arms, where graphical warning shields in Grummon et al. (2025) were serialized into text without visual warning cues.

Generalization to unseen shopper attributes. To test whether fine-tuning merely memorizes training-corpus demographics, we evaluate each model when prompted with one shopper attribute at a time across the \(6\) seen demographics and the \(7\) unseen covariates (Figure 3, Panel B; Table W5). Across individual attributes, off-the-shelf Kev 9B (base) varies widely in cross-entropy (1.3935 to 1.6661 bits) and performs worse than uniform guessing (1.5850 bits) on Nutrition Facts label usage (1.6661) and Share of groceries bought online (1.6016). If survey fine-tuning memorized only in-corpus demographics, improvements would be confined to the \(6\) seen attributes. Instead, Kev 9B (FT) lowers cross-entropy on every attribute, converging to virtually identical mean cross-entropy on the \(7\) unseen covariates (1.3473 bits, vs. 1.5328 for Kev 9B (base)) and the \(6\) seen demographics (1.3477 bits, vs. 1.4866 for Kev 9B (base)).

Discussion and Conclusion

Our findings show that the severe econometric biases in generative LLM silicon sampling (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026) stem from discrete text generation and single-label post-training rather than fundamental limits of foundation models. Whereas recent marketing methods correct fixed LLM outputs after inference using target-study human data (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026), aligning models before inference requires pairing a probabilistic readout with distributional supervision. Architecturally, single-pass decision heads output normalized probabilities directly over valid options, eliminating free-text parsing errors, Monte Carlo sampling variance, safety refusals on sensitive opt-out choices, and the nearly \(23\)-fold MNL error inflation caused by discretizing choices. Substantively, minimizing cross-entropy against empirical sample distributions across 1.88 million general survey responses (US ISSP and Twin-2K-500) calibrates conditional choice probabilities at unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)), generalizing zero-shot across unseen respondent covariates and substantially improving conjoint MNL and experimental OLS parameter recovery.

For marketing researchers, distributionally aligned decision models enable fast, deterministic pre-testing before primary data collection (e.g., screening candidate conjoint attributes, auditing survey instruments, or evaluating policy messaging across demographic subgroups in a single forward pass per task). At the same time, zero-shot simulation complements rather than replaces human experimentation for high-stakes managerial or regulatory decisions, as Kev 9B (FT) still exhibits higher estimation error than a 200-participant human sample (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0267\) vs. \(0.0065\) in conjoint MNL; \(0.0022\) vs. \(0.0016\) in trial OLS). When researchers do collect primary human responses in the target study, upstream distributional alignment and downstream statistical calibration (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026) are naturally complementary. Passing refusal-free, persona-conditioned probability vectors from Kev 9B (FT) into AI-augmented estimators (Wang et al. 2026) or checkpoint ensembles (Leng et al. 2026) could inject calibrated respondent-level variation while reducing reliance on large human calibration samples.

Three boundary conditions define priorities for future research. First, our nutrition trial highlights a modality boundary of text-only silicon sampling, as serializing graphical High In warning shields into text loses visual salience cues. Extending distributional training to multimodal vision-language decision heads that score package mockups and advertisements directly is an immediate next step. Second, because our training corpus centers on general US social and behavioral attitudes, Kev 9B (FT) attenuates unfamiliar clinical attributes (Duration and Major side effects) toward zero, and smaller models (0.8B and 4B) do not improve OLS MSE. Incorporating historical commercial conjoint studies, scanner panels, and cross-national surveys would broaden domain transfer. Third, conditioning solely on static persona covariates \(\mathbf{s}_i\) treats repeated choices per respondent as conditionally independent, motivating sequential conditioning on prior choices or integration with mixed-logit random-coefficients models. Together, single-pass decision architectures and distributional survey training provide a practical, extensible foundation for behavioral simulation in marketing science.
