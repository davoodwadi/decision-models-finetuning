\title{Distributional Silicon Sampling: Training Decision Models to
Predict Human Response Distributions}
\author{Davood Wadi \and Yu Ma}

\textbf{Abstract}

Replacing human respondents with generative large language models (LLMs)
in silicon sampling severely biases downstream econometric inference
because autoregressive post-training collapses choice uncertainty,
inflates regression coefficients, and triggers safety refusals. We
propose distributional silicon sampling using single-pass decision
models that score valid options directly in representation space and
output normalized choice probabilities without text generation. Because
off-the-shelf decision models are trained on single-label objective
benchmarks and remain overconfident on subjective choices, we fine-tune
open-weight decision models (\texttt{Kev\ 0.8B}, \texttt{4B}, and
\texttt{9B}) via grouped shared-prefix packing on 1.88 million
individual respondent-question records from US social and behavioral
surveys (\texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500}) across 17
harmonized attributes. Minimizing respondent-level cross-entropy
calibrates conditional choice distributions at unit temperature without
target-domain human data. Fine-tuning reduces held-out survey
cross-entropy by up to \(27.5\%\) and transfers zero-shot to two
out-of-domain marketing benchmarks. In COVID-19 vaccine conjoint
analysis, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} eliminates safety refusals, outperforms
generative LLMs by orders of magnitude, and reduces Multinomial Logit
part-worth MSE by \(76\%\) to \(78\%\) over off-the-shelf
\texttt{Kev\ 9B}. In a six-arm nutrition labeling randomized trial,
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} improves calibration across seen and unseen shopper
covariates and recovers causal OLS effects on interpretive labels while
delineating modality limits on visual warnings.

\textbf{Keywords.} Decision models, Silicon sampling, Econometric
inference, Conjoint analysis, Marketing research

\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Simulating consumer choices, attitudes, and survey responses with
foundation models, commonly referred to as silicon sampling, has
attracted growing interest in marketing, public opinion, and behavioral
research (Argyle et al. 2023, Brand et al. 2023, Horton et al. 2023,
Goli and Singh 2024, Sarstedt et al. 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al.
2026). In marketing applications, the primary goal is often downstream
econometric inference, such as estimating part-worth utilities in
conjoint analysis or causal treatment effects in randomized experiments.
Currently, researchers rely on generative large language models (LLMs)
that produce text one token at a time. However, recent studies in
marketing science establish that naively replacing human respondents
with generative LLM outputs introduces severe bias in preference
measurement and downstream regressions (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al.
2026, Wang et al. 2026). Because generative LLMs are post-trained for
deterministic reasoning and safety compliance, scaling up model size,
enabling chain-of-thought (\texttt{CoT}) reasoning, or fine-tuning
autoregressive models on small task demonstrations makes their choices
overly rational (Wang et al. 2026). This collapses human choice
diversity, triggers safety refusals on sensitive options (e.g., opting
out of vaccination in Wang et al. (2026)), and worsens regression
parameter recovery. Furthermore, estimating choice shares from text
generation requires costly Monte Carlo sampling (Wadi and Fredette 2025,
Zahradnickova et al. 2026). Recognizing that silicon sampling must be
treated as a calibration problem rather than a one-shot prompting
exercise (Leng et al. 2026), pioneering studies develop post-hoc
statistical methods that use human benchmark data to debias regression
estimates (Wang et al. 2026) or optimally weight ensembles of prompts,
personas, and model checkpoints (Leng et al. 2026). At the same time,
both studies highlight critical open directions for future research,
namely how to incorporate rich individual-level personas, improve the
upstream alignment of the foundation model itself, and extend LLM-based
inference to settings where target-domain human calibration data are
scarce or unavailable (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026).

Decision models, also known as System One architectures (Deußer et al.
2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Li and Wagle 2026, Palmer 2026), offer a
direct alternative for silicon sampling. Instead of generating text
tokens, a decision model takes a respondent persona and a structured
question (e.g., a categorical choice task or an ordinal Likert scale)
and outputs a normalized probability distribution over the supplied
options in a single prefill forward pass. Because the model scores each
option directly from its contextualized representation and restricts
probability mass strictly to the valid options, it eliminates
text-generation variance, Monte Carlo sampling overhead, and safety
refusals. Moreover, its continuous choice probabilities can be directly
plugged into maximum likelihood and ordinary least squares regressions.

Despite this architectural advantage, off-the-shelf decision models,
similar to generative LLMs, suffer from a fundamental mismatch between
their training objective and the goal of silicon sampling. Both model
families are trained to maximize single-answer accuracy on objective
benchmarks such as mathematics, code verification, and factual question
answering (Gao et al. 2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Palmer 2026). Standard
training pairs each prompt with a single class label and minimizes
cross-entropy loss to push all probability mass onto that one winning
option. In consumer and social surveys, however, respondents who share a
demographic profile never agree unanimously, and instead spread their
choices across options. A model trained to concentrate probability mass
on a single answer treats genuine within-group behavioral variation as
classification error, producing overconfident probabilities and biased
downstream regression estimates.

To resolve this training mismatch, we fine-tune open-weight decision
models (Palmer 2026), built on Qwen 3.5 backbones (Qwen Team 2026),
directly on respondent-level survey microdata and test whether this
training transfers zero-shot to downstream econometric inference. Our
training corpus combines 10 waves of the US International Social Survey
Programme (ISSP 2015 to 2024; 14,356 respondents and 616 questions) with
the Twin-2K-500 US panel (Toubia et al. 2025; 2,058 respondents and 974
questions), totaling 1,881,950 individual respondent-question
observations across 1,572 training questions and 17 harmonized
demographic and attitudinal attributes. Using grouped shared-prefix
training, we encode each respondent's multi-attribute persona once and
minimize cross-entropy loss across their packed individual responses.
This trains the decision model to predict calibrated conditional choice
distributions at unit temperature (\(T = 1\)), requiring no post-hoc
temperature tuning and no target-domain human calibration data.

We evaluate off-the-shelf and fine-tuned decision models across held-out
survey microdata and two out-of-domain marketing regression benchmarks.
First, on 13,183 respondent-question records from 18 held-out ISSP and
Twin-2K-500 validation questions, fine-tuning reduces respondent-level
cross-entropy by \(27.5\%\) (from \(2.398\) to \(1.738\) bits for Kev
9B). Second, we follow the empirical benchmark and Multinomial Logit
(MNL) evaluation protocol of Wang et al. (2026) on the Kreps et al.
(2020) COVID-19 vaccine conjoint dataset (\emph{N} = 1,971 respondents
and 9,855 choice sets across \(d = 11\) attribute levels). We evaluate
parameter recovery (\texttt{MAPE} and \texttt{MSE} against full-sample
human \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\)) on a 1,000-choice-set subsample under
both Wang et al. (2026)'s random-persona prompt and actual respondent
personas. Consistent with Wang et al. (2026), generative LLMs
(\texttt{Gemini\ 3.5\ Flash-Lite} and \texttt{Gemini\ 4\ Argon}) exhibit
severe estimation error (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.59\) to \(26.22\),
\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 203.6\%\) to \(1507.7\%\)) that worsens at higher
thinking budgets and is compounded by safety refusals. Off-the-shelf
\texttt{Kev\ 9B} (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0932\) to \(0.0982\)) already
outperforms the generative LLMs by an order of magnitude. Fine-tuned
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} reduces MNL MSE by a further \(76\%\) to \(78\%\),
reaching \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0205\) (\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 51.8\%\)) under
random personas and \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0239\)
(\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 56.6\%\)) under respondent personas, approaching the
finite-sample error of the 200-respondent human subsample itself
(\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0065\), \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.1\%\)). Third, we test
zero-shot generalization to a randomized clinical trial of
front-of-package nutrition labels (Grummon et al. 2025; \emph{N} = 5,633
US grocery shoppers and 33,798 product healthfulness choices across 6
labeling arms). Fine-tuning substantially reduces respondent-level
choice cross-entropy and accurately recovers the causal treatment-arm
effects in participant-level OLS regressions, even when conditioning on
consumer nutrition attributes never seen during training.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec-related}

\subsection{Silicon Sampling and Econometric Inference with Generative
LLMs}\label{silicon-sampling-and-econometric-inference-with-generative-llms}

Early work in silicon sampling prompted generative LLMs with demographic
backstories to simulate human responses in political, economic, and
marketing surveys (Argyle et al. 2023, Brand et al. 2023, Horton et al.
2023, Santurkar et al. 2023, Durmus et al. 2024, Goli and Singh 2024,
Sarstedt et al. 2024). Although generative LLMs often capture the
direction of average preferences, prior studies document systematic
discrepancies between LLM samples and human populations. Common failure
modes include mode collapse onto a single option, flattened within-group
variance, demographic stereotyping, and sensitivity to prompt formatting
(Santurkar et al. 2023, Koo et al. 2024, Shu et al. 2024, Wadi and
Fredette 2025, Deas and McKeown 2025, Kabir et al. 2025, Zahradnickova
et al. 2026, Jung et al. 2026). In marketing applications, these
distortions cause severe parameter bias when LLM responses are used for
downstream econometric estimation (Goli and Singh 2024, Leng et al.
2026, Wang et al. 2026). In choice-based conjoint analysis, Wang et al.
(2026) show that relying solely on AI choices or naively pooling them
with human data biases Multinomial Logit (MNL) part-worth estimates.
They also show that more capable LLMs and chain-of-thought
(\texttt{CoT}) prompting worsen estimation error by making choices
overly rational.

To address these discrepancies when the language model is treated as a
black-box generator, recent marketing studies develop principled
post-hoc calibration and data-augmentation frameworks that leverage
human benchmark data. Building on prediction-powered inference
(Angelopoulos, Bates, et al. 2023, Angelopoulos, Duchi, et al. 2023),
Wang et al. (2026) propose an AI-augmented estimator (AAE) for
choice-based conjoint analysis. Using a primary sample of human conjoint
responses alongside a larger auxiliary sample of LLM choices, AAE trains
a correction network over product features and AI choices to achieve
consistent, asymptotically normal parameter estimates and substantial
survey data savings. Importantly, Wang et al. (2026) show that even when
\texttt{GPT-4o} is fine-tuned on 50 target-domain conjoint
demonstrations, standalone AI estimation still increases parameter error
relative to the primary human sample because discrete token generation
collapses choice uncertainty. They further demonstrate that downstream
augmentation improves as the upstream AI generator becomes more
informative, and they explicitly call for future research into
incorporating multi-attribute respondent personas and extending
AI-assisted estimation to domains where primary target data are scarce.
Complementing downstream parameter correction, Leng et al. (2026)
propose Human Mimicry Calibration (HuMCal), which calibrates the LLM
measurement variable directly before downstream analysis. HuMCal solves
a convex simplex optimization to learn nonnegative weights over a
candidate set of LLM elicitation variants (e.g., demographic personas,
prompt templates, or model checkpoints) against an aggregate human
benchmark, guaranteeing in-sample baseline safety over fixed weighting
rules and demonstrating positive empirical transfer across related time
periods, regions, industries, and public opinion question clusters. In
outlining directions for future research, Leng et al. (2026) note the
bias-variance trade-off that arises when estimating simplex weights over
a growing number of discrete subgroup variants, the need for reliable
transfer when target calibration benchmarks are unavailable, and the
benefit of upgrading the underlying model checkpoints (which their
Proposition A-7 shows weakly improves calibration loss under uniform
dominance across calibration items).

Our work builds directly on the insights of Wang et al. (2026) and Leng
et al. (2026) and addresses the future research directions identified by
both studies. Whereas AAE and HuMCal calibrate frozen LLM outputs
downstream using target-domain or aggregate human benchmarks, we resolve
the calibration problem upstream by changing both the model architecture
and the training objective. In natural language processing, recent
studies fine-tune causal language models on aggregated group survey
distributions (Suh et al. 2025, Ku et al. 2026, Huang et al. 2026). We
advance beyond both black-box post-hoc calibration and group-level
language model fine-tuning in three ways. First, we shift from
autoregressive text generation to single-pass decision models that
directly output exact probability distributions over supplied options
without Monte Carlo sampling overhead or safety refusals (allowing us,
for example, to retain the vaccine opt-out respondents that Wang et al.
(2026) had to exclude due to commercial LLM safety filters). Second,
instead of estimating separate weights over a small grid of coarse
subgroups (Leng et al. 2026) or fine-tuning on a small task-specific
demonstration set (Wang et al. 2026), we train on \(1.88\) million
respondent-level microdata records from general surveys across 17
harmonized attributes. This amortizes calibration across combinatorial
respondent personas in representation space and answers Wang et al.
(2026)'s call for granular persona conditioning. Third, we demonstrate
that upstream respondent-level training transfers zero-shot to unseen
conjoint MNL (Wang et al. 2026) and randomized trial OLS (Grummon et al.
2025) regressions when no target-domain human data are available, while
motivating future research on pairing refusal-free, persona-conditioned
decision-model probabilities with downstream frameworks such as AAE or
HuMCal when target human benchmarks are collected.

\subsection{Decision Models and Direct Probability
Readouts}\label{decision-models-and-direct-probability-readouts}

To avoid the cost and sampling variance of free-text generation, several
studies extract next-token probabilities over option labels from causal
LLMs ({Kadavath et al.} 2022, Kuribayashi et al. 2024, Bentegeac et al.
2025, Wadi et al. 2026). Decision models extend this idea into a
dedicated single-pass architecture (Asseri and Asseri 2026, Deußer et
al. 2026, Geuter et al. 2026, Li 2026, Li and Wagle 2026, Palmer 2026).
Built on pretrained language model backbones (Qwen Team 2026), decision
models compile a context state and typed questions (\texttt{score} for
ordered rating scales and \texttt{choice} for categorical options) into
a structured prompt. They then compute option probabilities in one
prefill pass. Vocabulary-letter readouts map options to arbitrary token
symbols (e.g., ``A'', ``B'', and ``C'') and remain susceptible to
token-frequency priors. By contrast, a decision model scores each option
directly from its contextualized text representation and restricts
probability mass strictly to the supplied options (Palmer 2026).
However, existing decision models are trained on single-label objective
benchmarks. As a result, they exhibit severe probability compression on
subjective and ordinal tasks (Gao et al. 2026), which motivates our
respondent-level training framework.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec-methodology}

\subsection{Predicting Respondent-Level Choice
Probabilities}\label{predicting-respondent-level-choice-probabilities}

Consider a survey or experimental dataset of human respondents indexed
by \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Each respondent has a multi-attribute
persona state \(\mathbf{s}_i\) (e.g., sex, age, education, income,
political party, and race) and answers a set of questions
\(\mathcal{Q}_i\). Each question \(q \in \mathcal{Q}_i\) presents
\(K_q\) discrete answer options
\(\mathcal{Y}_q = \{y_1, \dots, y_{K_q}\}\). For categorical
\texttt{choice} items, the options are unordered alternatives that may
include attribute descriptions. For ordinal \texttt{score} items, the
options are ordered rating levels
\(y_1 \prec y_2 \prec \dots \prec y_{K_q}\). Let
\(\mathbf{y}_{i,q} = (y_{i,q,1}, \dots, y_{i,q,K_q}) \in \{0, 1\}^{K_q}\)
denote respondent \(i\)'s observed one-hot choice vector on question
\(q\), where \(y_{i,q,k} = 1\) if respondent \(i\) selected option
\(y_k\) and \(0\) otherwise.

In a decision model (Palmer 2026), the persona state \(\mathbf{s}_i\),
question text \(q\), and options \(\mathcal{Y}_q\) are formatted into a
structured prompt and processed in one causal forward pass. Let
\(\mathbf{h}_{i,q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) denote the
final-layer hidden state at the closing token of question \(q\). Let
\(\mathbf{h}_{i,q,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) denote the
final-layer hidden state at the closing token of option \(k\). Using
learned linear projections
\(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}} \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}\),
the decision head computes a score \(z_{i,q,k}\) for each option \(k\):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-pointer-head}{
z_{i,q,k} = \frac{(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}_{i,q})^\top (\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{i,q,k})}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}.
}\end{equation} Applying a softmax function over the \(K_q\) option
scores yields the predicted choice distribution in a single pass:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-model-pmf}{
P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}_i) = \frac{\exp(z_{i,q,k} / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K_q} \exp(z_{i,q,j} / T)},
}\end{equation} where \(T\) is the readout temperature (\(T = 1\) for
all fine-tuned models, with no post-hoc temperature fitting).

\subsection{Respondent-Level Training and Grouped Shared-Prefix
Packing}\label{respondent-level-training-and-grouped-shared-prefix-packing}

To train a decision model to approximate human choice behavior
conditional on a persona, we optimize model parameters \(\theta\)
directly on individual respondent-question observations \((i, q)\).
Specifically, we minimize respondent-level cross-entropy loss (measured
in bits) against the observed one-hot response \(\mathbf{y}_{i,q}\):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-resp-ce}{
\mathcal{L}_{i,q}(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_q} y_{i,q,k} \log_2 P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{i,q} = y_k \mid \mathbf{s}_i; \theta).
}\end{equation} By the law of total expectation, averaging
\(\mathcal{L}_{i,q}(\theta)\) across all respondents who share persona
state \(\mathbf{s}\) equals the cross-entropy between the empirical
conditional human distribution
\(P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s})\) and
\(P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta)\). Minimizing
Equation~\ref{eq-resp-ce} is therefore equivalent to minimizing the
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}) \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta))\)
up to the fixed entropy of the human responses. Questions on which
respondents with the same persona naturally disagree are thus learned as
spread probability distributions rather than single-class labels.

In respondent-level survey data, each participant \(i\) typically
answers dozens of questions \(\mathcal{Q}_i\) while sharing the same
persona state \(\mathbf{s}_i\). Encoding \(\mathbf{s}_i\) separately for
every question is computationally redundant. To accelerate training and
evaluation, we use grouped shared-prefix packing. We partition each
respondent's questions into groups of up to \(M_{\max}\) questions
(bounded by a maximum character budget \(C_{\max}\)). The model encodes
the persona prefix \(\mathbf{s}_i\) once and scores all questions in the
group against the cached state prefix in parallel. During training, we
randomly permute the order of demographic attributes in \(\mathbf{s}_i\)
for each group so that the model is invariant to attribute ordering.

\subsection{Downstream Econometric Estimation from Model
Predictions}\label{downstream-econometric-estimation-from-model-predictions}

To evaluate whether decision models can replace human respondents in
applied marketing research, we feed the models' predicted choice
probabilities directly into the econometric regressions of two
out-of-domain studies.

\subsubsection{Multinomial Logit (MNL) in Conjoint
Analysis}\label{multinomial-logit-mnl-in-conjoint-analysis}

We follow the choice-based conjoint setup and evaluation protocol of
Wang et al. (2026). Each respondent \(i\) evaluates
\(t \in \{1, \dots, 5\}\) paired choice tasks. Each task presents two
hypothetical product profiles (Option A and Option B) with binary
attribute vectors
\(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}, \mathbf{x}_{i,t,B} \in \{0, 1\}^d\), alongside an
outside no-choice option (Option C) whose utility is normalized to zero.
Under the standard MNL specification (Wang et al. 2026), the choice
probabilities for part-worth parameter vector
\(\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-mnl-probs}{
P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}, \qquad P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,A}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \exp(\mathbf{x}_{i,t,B}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta})},
}\end{equation} and
\(P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 1 - P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) - P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta})\).
Given observed or predicted choice shares
\((p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})\) across \(n\) choice sets, we
estimate \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) by minimizing the negative
log-likelihood via Newton-CG:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-mnl-mle}{
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^d} -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{(i,t)} \Big[ p_{i,t,A} \log P_A(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) + p_{i,t,B} \log P_B(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) + p_{i,t,C} \log P_C(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}; \boldsymbol{\beta}) \Big].
}\end{equation} For human respondents and generative LLMs,
\((p_{i,t,A}, p_{i,t,B}, p_{i,t,C})\) is a one-hot choice vector. For
decision models, we use the continuous choice probabilities output by
Equation~\ref{eq-model-pmf}. Following Wang et al. (2026), let
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) denote the ground-truth MNL parameter vector
estimated on the full human dataset. We evaluate parameter recovery
using Mean Absolute Percentage Error (\(\mathrm{MAPE}\)) and Mean
Squared Error (\(\mathrm{MSE}\)) across the \(d\) attributes:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-wang-metrics}{
\mathrm{MAPE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{|\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j|}{|\beta^*_j|} \times 100\%, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} (\hat{\beta}_j - \beta^*_j)^2.
}\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Participant-Level OLS in Randomized Clinical
Trials}\label{participant-level-ols-in-randomized-clinical-trials}

In our second econometric study, we evaluate causal treatment-effect
recovery in the front-of-package nutrition labeling randomized clinical
trial of Grummon et al. (2025). Each shopper \(i\) is randomized to one
of six labeling arms \(a_i\) (the reference \texttt{Positive} arm or one
of five treatment arms) and completes six 3-option product healthfulness
choice tasks \(q \in \{1, \dots, 6\}\). In each task, option \(y_1\) is
the objectively healthier food or beverage product. For each shopper
\(i\), we compute the mean probability of selecting the healthier
product across the six categories,
\(\bar{p}_i = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{q=1}^6 P(Y_{i,q} = y_1 \mid \mathbf{s}_i, a_i)\).
Following Grummon et al. (2025), we estimate the five treatment-arm
effects \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \in \mathbb{R}^5\) relative to the
\texttt{Positive} reference arm via ordinary least squares (OLS) with
HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. We compare the
model-estimated treatment effects \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) against
the human RCT treatment effects \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) using
\(\mathrm{OLS\ MAPE}\), \(\mathrm{OLS\ MSE}\), \(\mathrm{OLS\ MAE}\),
and Spearman rank correlation \(\rho\).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec-experiments}

\subsection{Harmonized Respondent-Level Training Corpus (US ISSP and
Twin-2K-500)}\label{harmonized-respondent-level-training-corpus-us-issp-and-twin-2k-500}

\textbf{Survey microdata sources.} To train a general-purpose
respondent-conditioned decision model without relying on target-domain
marketing data, we construct a harmonized microdata corpus from two
public US individual-level survey sources (Table~\ref{tbl-datasets}).
The first source comprises \(10\) consecutive US waves of the
International Social Survey Programme (\texttt{US\ ISSP}, 2015 to 2024;
\quartoapptblref{apptbl-issp-waves}), spanning diverse thematic modules
on work orientations, the role of government, social networks, religion,
social inequality, the environment, health and health care, family and
gender roles, national identity, and digital societies. Across these
\(10\) waves, we extract 767,484 individual responses from 14,356 unique
US respondents across \(616\) survey questions (\(474\) ordinal Likert
\texttt{score} items and \(142\) unordered categorical \texttt{choice}
items, with a mean of \(53.5\) answered questions per respondent). The
second source is the \texttt{Twin-2K-500} digital twin benchmark (Toubia
et al. 2025), from which we extract 1,127,649 individual responses
across 2,058 US participants and \(974\) psychological, economic, and
behavioral questions (\(287\) ordinal Likert \texttt{score} items and
\(687\) categorical \texttt{choice} items, with a mean of \(547.9\)
answered questions per respondent). Combined across both sources, the
full survey corpus contains 1,895,133 respondent-question observations
from 16,414 unique US respondents (16,338 distinct persona
configurations) across 1,590 questions.

\textbf{Question-level training and validation split.} To evaluate
respondent-level distributional calibration on unseen survey questions
prior to out-of-domain econometric testing, we partition the survey
corpus at the question level (Table~\ref{tbl-datasets}). The training
split contains 1,881,950 respondent-question observations across 1,572
questions (\(757\) Likert \texttt{score} items and \(815\) categorical
\texttt{choice} items) from all 16,414 respondents (760,473 observations
across \(612\) questions from \texttt{US\ ISSP} and 1,121,477
observations across \(960\) questions from \texttt{Twin-2K-500}). The
held-out validation split comprises 13,183 respondent-question
observations across \(18\) completely unseen questions from 3,885
respondents, consisting of \(4\) ordinal Likert \texttt{score} questions
from the 2020 \texttt{US\ ISSP} wave (7,011 observations across 1,827
respondents) and \(14\) categorical \texttt{choice} questions from
\texttt{Twin-2K-500} (6,172 observations across 2,058 respondents).
Because the split is performed strictly by question, none of the
validation items or their question stems appear in the training set.

\textbf{Harmonized persona attributes.} Each respondent \(i\) in the
training and validation corpus is represented by a structured persona
vector \(\mathbf{s}_i\) drawn from \(17\) harmonized demographic and
attitudinal attributes (\quartoapptblref{apptbl-attributes}). Of these
\(17\) attributes, \(11\) are shared across both \texttt{US\ ISSP} and
\texttt{Twin-2K-500}, namely sex, age, education, religion, attendance
of religious services, political ideology, marital status, main
activity, political party, household income, and US census region. Four
additional attributes are recorded exclusively in \texttt{US\ ISSP}
(voting participation in the last general election, urban-rural place of
living, trade union membership, and ancestry), and two attributes are
recorded exclusively in \texttt{Twin-2K-500} (race and household size).
Because the 16,414 respondents span 16,338 distinct persona
configurations (nearly one respondent per cell), discrete subgroup
reweighting (Leng et al. 2026) cannot be applied at the individual
level; instead, conditioning a shared decision model on the factorized
attribute text amortizes calibration across combinatorial profiles. When
a given attribute is unrecorded or missing for respondent \(i\), that
attribute line is omitted from the conditioning prompt so that the
decision model learns to marginalize consistently over partially
observed respondent profiles.

\subsection{Out-of-Domain Marketing Evaluation
Benchmarks}\label{out-of-domain-marketing-evaluation-benchmarks}

\textbf{Choice-based conjoint analysis of COVID-19 vaccine preferences.}
Our first out-of-domain marketing evaluation tests structural part-worth
utility recovery on the choice-based conjoint dataset of Kreps et al.
(2020), following the benchmark setup of Wang et al. (2026)
(Table~\ref{tbl-datasets}). In this study, 1,971 US respondents each
evaluate \(5\) discrete choice sets (9,855 total choice observations).
In each choice set, the respondent chooses between two hypothetical
COVID-19 vaccine profiles (\texttt{Vaccine\ A} and \texttt{Vaccine\ B})
or selects the no-vaccine opt-out alternative (\texttt{Neither}). Each
vaccine profile is randomized across \(7\) attributes represented by
\(d = 11\) non-reference binary indicator levels: efficacy (\(70\%\) and
\(90\%\) relative to a \(50\%\) baseline), protection duration (\(5\)
years relative to \(1\) year), incidence of major side effects (\(1\) in
1,000,000 relative to \(1\) in 10,000), incidence of minor side effects
(\(1\) in \(30\) relative to \(1\) in \(10\)), regulatory authorization
(emergency use authorization relative to full FDA approval), country of
origin (China and the United Kingdom relative to the United States), and
institutional endorsement (Vice President Biden, the US Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention, and the World Health Organization
relative to President Trump). Following Wang et al. (2026), the
multinomial logit (MNL) parameter vector
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* \in \mathbb{R}^{11}\) estimated on the full
9,855-observation human dataset serves as the ground-truth benchmark,
and model predictions are evaluated on the held-out subsample of \(200\)
respondents (\emph{n} = 1,000 choice sets). We evaluate each model under
both the unconditioned random-persona prompt of Wang et al. (2026) and
respondent-level persona conditioning using the \(8\) demographic and
political attributes recorded per participant (sex, age, education,
race, household income, religion, political party, and political
ideology), all \(8\) of which are included among the \(17\) training
attributes (\quartoapptblref{apptbl-attributes}). This comparison
directly tests the future research direction proposed by Wang et al.
(2026) on incorporating participant-specific personas into conjoint
queries, while retaining all respondents who selected the no-vaccine
opt-out (\texttt{Neither}), whom Wang et al. (2026) had to exclude due
to commercial generative LLM safety refusals.

\textbf{Randomized clinical trial of front-of-package nutrition
labeling.} Our second out-of-domain evaluation tests causal
treatment-effect recovery on the front-of-package nutrition labeling
randomized clinical trial of Grummon et al. (2025)
(Table~\ref{tbl-datasets}). In this experiment, \emph{N} = 5,633 US
grocery shoppers are randomly assigned to one of \(6\) between-subjects
labeling arms (\texttt{Positive} {[}reference arm{]},
\texttt{Nutrition\ Info}, \texttt{High\ In} warning,
\texttt{Positive\ +\ Nutrition\ Info}, \texttt{Positive\ +\ High\ In},
and \texttt{Spectrum} {[}1-to-5 star rating{]}) and complete \(6\)
three-option product healthfulness choice tasks across distinct food and
beverage categories, yielding \(36\) arm-by-category question cells and
33,798 individual product choices. Mirroring our conjoint benchmark, the
participant-level HC1 OLS treatment-effect vector
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* \in \mathbb{R}^5\) (relative to
\texttt{Positive}) estimated on the full 5,633-shopper dataset serves as
the ground-truth benchmark, and downstream parameter recovery is
evaluated on a random subsample of \(200\) shoppers (\emph{n} = 1,200
choice tasks) under both unconditioned random prompts and respondent
personas. Each shopper profile includes \(10\) demographic and
behavioral attributes (\quartoapptblref{apptbl-attributes}). Notably,
whereas \(5\) of these shopper attributes overlap with the survey
training corpus (sex, age, education, household income, and race), the
remaining \(5\) attributes are completely unseen during training
(Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, SNAP or Food Stamps participation,
Nutrition Facts label usage frequency, share of groceries purchased
online, and objective nutrition literacy score). This partial covariate
overlap allows us to test whether respondent-level fine-tuning
generalizes not only to unseen choice tasks and experimental arms, but
also to respondent profiles containing novel domain-specific behavioral
covariates.

\subsection{Models and Training
Configuration}\label{models-and-training-configuration}

\textbf{Off-the-shelf and respondent-fine-tuned decision models.} We
evaluate the \texttt{Kev} family of pretrained decision models (Gao et
al. 2026, Palmer 2026) across three parameter scales
(\texttt{Kev\ 0.8B}, \texttt{Kev\ 4B}, and \texttt{Kev\ 9B}), comparing
each off-the-shelf base checkpoint against its respondent-level
fine-tuned counterpart (\texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ FT}, \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ FT},
and \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT}). Each base model is adapted on the 1,881,950
training observations in \texttt{bfloat16} precision using Low-Rank
Adaptation (\texttt{LoRA}, rank \(r = 64\), \(\alpha = 128\)) applied to
all attention and feed-forward linear projections and the output scoring
head. To amortize encoding of the respondent persona prefix across
multiple answered items, we pack all training questions for a given
respondent into shared-prefix token groups and optimize respondent-level
cross-entropy (Equation~\ref{eq-resp-ce}) using AdamW with a peak
learning rate of \(5 \times 10^{-5}\), weight decay of \(0.01\), a
linear warmup of 2,000 steps followed by a cosine decay schedule,
gradient clipping at \(1.0\), and gradient accumulation over \(12\)
packed respondent groups per step. Models are evaluated every \(100\)
optimizer steps on the 13,183 held-out validation observations across
the \(18\) unseen \texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500} survey
questions, and the checkpoint achieving the lowest validation
cross-entropy is selected for out-of-domain evaluation. At inference
time, both off-the-shelf and fine-tuned decision models produce complete
option probability distributions in a single forward pass without any
target-domain demonstrations, using each off-the-shelf checkpoint's
released calibration temperature (\(T = 2.35\) for
\texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ (base)}, \(T = 2.41\) for \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ (base)},
and \(T = 2.19\) for \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)}) and unit temperature
(\(T = 1.00\)) with no post-hoc temperature tuning for all fine-tuned
models.

\textbf{Generative LLM and human subsample baselines.} On both
out-of-domain marketing benchmarks, we report the \(200\)-participant
human subsample baseline, which estimates the target econometric model
directly on the empirical choices of the \(200\) sampled human
participants (\emph{n} = 1,000 conjoint choice sets or \emph{n} = 1,200
nutrition trial tasks) relative to the full-sample parameter vector
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\). On the COVID-19 vaccine conjoint benchmark
(Kreps et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2026), we additionally evaluate frontier
and high-efficiency autoregressive generative LLMs
(\texttt{Gemini\ 4\ Argon} at \texttt{low} and \texttt{high} thinking
budgets, and \texttt{Gemini\ 3.5\ Flash-Lite} at \texttt{minimal} and
\texttt{high} thinking budgets) under both the unconditioned
random-persona prompt and the \(8\)-attribute respondent personas.

\section{Results}\label{sec-results}

\subsection{Validation on Held-Out Survey
Microdata}\label{validation-on-held-out-survey-microdata}

\textbf{Distributional calibration on unseen survey questions.} Before
examining out-of-domain econometric estimation, we evaluate whether
respondent-level fine-tuning improves conditional response distribution
recovery on the 13,183 held-out survey validation observations across
\(18\) unseen \texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500} questions
(Table~\ref{tbl-validation} and Figure~\ref{fig-validation-curves}).
Prior to fine-tuning, the off-the-shelf decision models exhibit high
respondent-level cross-entropy on the held-out survey questions
(\texttt{2.4262} bits for \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B}, \texttt{2.3586} bits for
\texttt{Kev\ 4B}, and \texttt{2.3983} bits for \texttt{Kev\ 9B}), with
negligible advantage from increasing model scale alone. Fine-tuning on
the 1,881,950 survey training records reduces held-out validation
cross-entropy by \(26.0\%\) to \(27.5\%\) across all three model scales,
reaching minima of \texttt{1.7963} bits on \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B} (at
\(0.34\) epochs), \texttt{1.7431} bits on \texttt{Kev\ 4B} (at \(0.67\)
epochs), and \texttt{1.7377} bits on \texttt{Kev\ 9B} (at \(0.60\)
epochs). As shown in Panel A of Figure~\ref{fig-validation-curves}, the
largest reduction in cross-entropy occurs during the initial \(0.10\)
epochs of training as the decision head adapts from generic pretraining
to human survey response scales, followed by steady refinement through
mid-epoch as the model learns demographic and attitudinal conditioning
across respondent personas.

\textbf{Zero-shot out-of-domain transfer during training.} To verify
that minimizing cross-entropy on general social and behavioral surveys
translates into genuine out-of-domain calibration rather than
memorization of survey-specific phrasing, we simultaneously track
zero-shot choice cross-entropy on all 33,798 product healthfulness
choices (\emph{N} = 5,633 shoppers across \(36\) arm-by-category tasks)
in the front-of-package nutrition labeling randomized clinical trial of
Grummon et al. (2025) (Table~\ref{tbl-validation} and Panel B of
Figure~\ref{fig-validation-curves}). Even though no food, beverage, or
nutrition labeling items appear anywhere in the training corpus,
zero-shot out-of-domain cross-entropy on the nutrition trial declines
monotonically alongside held-out survey validation loss during Epoch 1,
dropping by \(17.5\%\) on \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B} (\texttt{2.0224} to
\texttt{1.6686} bits), \(31.5\%\) on \texttt{Kev\ 4B} (\texttt{2.1745}
to \texttt{1.4899} bits), and \(25.9\%\) on \texttt{Kev\ 9B}
(\texttt{1.8494} to \texttt{1.3710} bits). Furthermore, whereas the
off-the-shelf models show weak and non-monotonic scaling on the held-out
survey validation set, respondent-level fine-tuning unlocks a clear
scaling hierarchy on out-of-domain marketing transfer, with
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} achieving substantially lower out-of-domain choice
cross-entropy (\texttt{1.3710} bits) than both \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ FT}
(\texttt{1.4899} bits) and \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ FT} (\texttt{1.6686}
bits).

\subsection{Multinomial Logit Estimation in Conjoint
Analysis}\label{multinomial-logit-estimation-in-conjoint-analysis}

\textbf{Part-worth recovery across model families, reasoning budgets,
and personas.} Table~\ref{tbl-wang-mnl} reports Multinomial Logit (MNL)
part-worth parameter recovery on the \(200\)-respondent COVID-19 vaccine
conjoint subsample (\emph{n} = 1,000 choice sets across \(d = 11\)
attribute indicators; see \quartoappfigref{appfig-wang-random} and
\quartoappfigref{appfig-wang-persona} for visual error comparisons),
benchmarked against the full-sample human ground-truth vector
\(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) (\emph{N} = 1,971 respondents, 9,855 choice
sets). Estimating the MNL model directly on the empirical choices of the
\(200\) sampled human respondents establishes the finite-sample
benchmark of \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.1\%\) and \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0065\).
Consistent with Wang et al. (2026), autoregressive generative LLMs
exhibit severe parameter bias under the unconditioned random-persona
prompt (\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 531.7\%\) to \(1507.7\%\);
\(\mathrm{MSE} = 3.2073\) to \(26.2179\)). Directly testing the
future-work hypothesis of Wang et al. (2026), conditioning generative
LLMs on the \(8\) individual demographic and political respondent
attributes reduces MSE by \(81.5\%\) to \(92.5\%\)
(\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.5925\) to \(2.1687\)), confirming that respondent
heterogeneity mitigates part of the aggregation distortion. Even with
granular respondent personas, however, generative LLMs still exceed the
MSE of the human subsample by \(91\) to \(334\) times
(\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 203.6\%\) to \(395.1\%\)). Furthermore, increasing
reasoning effort or model capability worsens econometric recovery across
both prompting regimes: raising the thinking budget on
\texttt{Gemini\ 3.5\ Flash-Lite} from \texttt{minimal} to \texttt{high}
increases MSE \(4.5\)-fold under random personas (\(3.2073\) to
\(14.4555\)) and \(2.0\)-fold under respondent personas (\(0.5925\) to
\(1.1984\)), while scaling to frontier \texttt{Gemini\ 4\ Argon}
increases MSE further to \(23.3998\) (\texttt{low}) and \(26.2179\)
(\texttt{high}) under random personas and \(1.7637\) (\texttt{low}) and
\(2.1687\) (\texttt{high}) under respondent personas.

\textbf{Why generative LLMs fail and why continuous decision readouts
succeed.} Inspection of the choice distributions and attribute-level
estimates (Figure~\ref{fig-wang-coefs}, Panel B, and
\quartoapptblref{apptbl-wang-coefs}) isolates two mechanisms behind
generative LLM distortion. First, whereas human respondents choose the
no-vaccine outside option (\texttt{Option\ C}) in \(21.0\%\) of
full-sample tasks (\(20.3\%\) in the subsample), unconditioned
generative LLMs almost never opt out of vaccination: \texttt{Option\ C}
share drops to \(3.0\%\) for
\texttt{Gemini\ 3.5\ Flash-Lite\ (minimal)}, \(0.3\%\) for
\texttt{Gemini\ 4\ Argon\ (low)}, and \(0.0\%\) at \texttt{high}
thinking for both models (and under respondent personas, raising
\texttt{Gemini\ 3.5\ Flash-Lite} thinking from \texttt{minimal} to
\texttt{high} compresses \texttt{Option\ C} share from \(21.9\%\) to
\(4.8\%\)). Combined with deterministic utility maximization across
paired profiles, this outside-option collapse inflates MNL log-odds
coefficients by an order of magnitude (e.g., under respondent personas,
\texttt{Efficacy:\ 90\%} reaches \(+1.846\) to \(+3.538\) versus human
\(+0.746\), and \texttt{Origin:\ China} reaches \(-1.908\) to \(-3.556\)
versus \(-0.570\)). At \texttt{high} thinking under random personas,
\texttt{Gemini\ 4\ Argon} additionally refuses to select an option on
\(16\) of \(1,000\) tasks (\emph{n} = 984), citing neutrality policies
on politically endorsed vaccine trade-offs. Second, replacing discrete
token generation with single-pass continuous probabilities from
off-the-shelf \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} (evaluated at its pretrained
calibration temperature \(T = 2.19\)) eliminates refusals across all
\(1,000\) tasks and lowers MNL MSE by an order of magnitude to
\(0.0932\) (\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 110.8\%\)) under random personas and
\(0.0982\) (\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 87.4\%\)) under respondent personas.
Notably, discretizing \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} predictions into one-hot
argmax choices degrades its respondent-persona MSE nearly eightfold
(\(0.0982\) to \(0.7761\)), demonstrating that plugging continuous
within-respondent probabilities directly into the MNL log-likelihood
(Equation~\ref{eq-mnl-mle}) is essential for preserving choice
uncertainty.

\textbf{Zero-shot gains and attribute-level alignment from respondent
fine-tuning.} Fine-tuning on general social and behavioral survey
microdata (\texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500}), without exposing
the model to any vaccine or conjoint examples and evaluating at unit
temperature (\(T = 1.00\)), substantially improves zero-shot conjoint
estimation (Table~\ref{tbl-wang-mnl}). Relative to off-the-shelf
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)}, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} reduces MNL MSE by
\(78.0\%\) under random personas (\(0.0932\) to \(0.0205\);
\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 51.8\%\)) and by \(75.7\%\) under respondent personas
(\(0.0982\) to \(0.0239\); \(\mathrm{MAPE} = 56.6\%\)), while reducing
respondent-level choice cross-entropy from \(1.6429\) to \(1.4990\) bits
(\(-8.8\%\)) and \(1.6598\) to \(1.5042\) bits (\(-9.4\%\)) and
maintaining realistic aggregate \texttt{Option\ C} probability mass
(\(24.8\%\) and \(32.2\%\)). As shown in Panel A of
Figure~\ref{fig-wang-coefs} (\quartoapptblref{apptbl-wang-coefs}),
off-the-shelf \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} still overstates high-salience
attributes (\texttt{Efficacy:\ 90\%} \(= +1.399\) vs.~\(+0.746\);
\texttt{Origin:\ China} \(= -0.808\) vs.~\(-0.570\);
\texttt{Endorsed:\ US\ CDC} \(= +0.722\) vs.~\(+0.416\)), reverses the
sign on \texttt{FDA:\ Emergency\ Use\ Authorization} (\(+0.024\)
vs.~\(-0.115\)), and places only \(4\) of \(11\) point estimates within
the \(95\%\) confidence intervals of the \(200\)-respondent human
subsample. By contrast, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} places \(9\) of \(11\)
point estimates inside the human subsample's \(95\%\) confidence
intervals, closely matching human part-worths on efficacy (\(+0.296\)
and \(+0.627\) vs.~\(+0.361\) and \(+0.746\)), Emergency Use
Authorization (\(-0.195\) vs.~\(-0.115\)), country of origin (\(-0.180\)
and \(-0.439\) vs.~\(-0.140\) and \(-0.570\)), and institutional
endorsements (\texttt{US\ CDC} \(+0.415\) vs.~\(+0.416\); \texttt{WHO}
\(+0.249\) vs.~\(+0.273\); \texttt{Biden} \(+0.036\) vs.~\(+0.095\)).
The remaining error is concentrated in conservative attenuation toward
zero on two clinical risk-horizon attributes
(\texttt{Duration:\ 5\ years} \(= -0.037\) vs.~\(+0.261\) and
\texttt{Major\ side\ effects:\ 1\ in\ 1,000,000} \(= -0.027\)
vs.~\(+0.304\)), indicating that general survey training calibrates
overall preference scale, origin, and institutional trust without
inducing the extreme coefficient inflation of generative LLMs.

\subsection{Causal Treatment-Effect Recovery in the Nutrition Labeling
Randomized
Trial}\label{causal-treatment-effect-recovery-in-the-nutrition-labeling-randomized-trial}

\textbf{Respondent-level choice calibration and causal treatment-effect
recovery across scales.} Table~\ref{tbl-grummon2025-ols} reports
three-option choice cross-entropy (in bits, matching
Equation~\ref{eq-resp-ce}) and participant-level HC1 OLS
treatment-effect recovery across the \(d = 5\) front-of-package (FOP)
labeling arms relative to the \texttt{Positive} reference arm on the
\(200\)-shopper evaluation subsample (\emph{n} = 1,200 product
healthfulness choices; see \quartoappfigref{appfig-grummon2025-random}
and \quartoappfigref{appfig-grummon2025-persona} for visual error
comparisons), benchmarked against the full-sample human treatment-effect
vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}^*\) (\emph{N} = 5,633 shoppers, 33,798
choices). Estimating the participant-level OLS regression directly on
the \(200\) sampled human shoppers yields the finite-sample benchmark of
\(\mathrm{MAPE} = 22.7\%\) and \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0016\) (with Spearman
\(\rho = +0.40\) across the \(5\) arms because finite-sample noise swaps
the nearly tied \texttt{Nutrition\ Info} {[}\(+0.184\){]} and
\texttt{Positive\ +\ Nutrition\ Info} {[}\(+0.179\){]} arms). Under
\(10\)-attribute respondent-persona conditioning, all three
off-the-shelf base models---even at their released calibration
temperatures (\(T = 2.35\), \(2.41\), and \(2.19\))---perform worse than
uniform three-option guessing (\(\log_2 3 = 1.5850\) bits) on individual
choice cross-entropy (\texttt{1.6132} bits for
\texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ (base)}, \texttt{1.6966} bits for
\texttt{Kev\ 4B\ (base)}, and \texttt{1.6196} bits for
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)}). Adding multi-attribute shopper personas
actually degrades off-the-shelf \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ (base)}
(\texttt{1.3983} to \texttt{1.6966} bits; \(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0021\) to
\(0.0076\)) and \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} (\texttt{1.6011} to
\texttt{1.6196} bits) relative to unconditioned random prompts. By
contrast, zero-shot survey fine-tuning (\(T = 1.00\)) establishes
monotonic scaling in respondent-conditioned choice calibration
(\texttt{1.6602} bits on \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ FT}, \texttt{1.5052} bits on
\texttt{Kev\ 4B\ FT} {[}\(-11.3\%\){]}, and \texttt{1.3860} bits on
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} {[}\(-14.4\%\){]}, closely matching the full-sample
\texttt{1.3710} bits in Table~\ref{tbl-validation}). On downstream OLS
parameter recovery, \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ FT} reverses the negative rank
correlation of \texttt{Kev\ 0.8B\ (base)} (\(\rho = -0.40\) to \(+0.20\)
under respondent personas; \(-0.30\) to \(+0.50\) under random personas)
and reduces MSE by \(30.4\%\) under random personas (\(0.0112\) to
\(0.0078\)) and \(15.5\%\) under respondent personas (\(0.0116\) to
\(0.0098\)). At the largest scale, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} achieves
\(\rho = +0.90\), reduces random-persona MSE by \(18.8\%\) (\(0.0069\)
to \(0.0056\)), and matches \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} in overall
respondent-persona MSE (\(0.0056\) vs.~\(0.0051\)) while substantially
improving individual choice calibration. At the intermediate
\(4\text{B}\) scale, although fine-tuning reduces respondent-persona
cross-entropy by \(11.3\%\) and raises \(\rho\) from \(+0.80\) to
\(+0.90\), lifting baseline \texttt{Positive}-arm accuracy (\(25.7\%\)
to \(33.1\%\)) compresses between-arm differences and increases OLS MSE
to \(0.0122\).

\textbf{Interpretive versus asymmetric warning labels in text-only
experimental arms.} Examining the arm-level estimates under respondent
personas (Panel A of Figure~\ref{fig-grummon2025-coefs} and
\quartoapptblref{apptbl-grummon2025-coefs}) clarifies both the strengths
and the modality boundaries of text-based silicon sampling. First,
off-the-shelf \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} assigns higher probability mass
to the \emph{less} healthy product than to the healthier product in
\(4\) of the \(6\) experimental arms (e.g., \(34.2\%\) healthier versus
\(43.9\%\) less healthy in \texttt{Positive}, and \(34.7\%\) versus
\(46.2\%\) in \texttt{Spectrum}), resulting in a near-zero treatment
effect on the \texttt{Spectrum} 1-to-5 star rating (\(+0.005\) vs.~human
\(+0.108\)). Fine-tuning on survey microdata restores realistic baseline
choice and tie shares (e.g.,
\texttt{"They\textquotesingle{}re\ the\ same"} share of \(30.0\%\)
vs.~human \(31.6\%\) in \texttt{Positive}) and accurately recovers
causal treatment effects across all three \textbf{symmetrical
interpretive labeling arms}, improving over \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} on
every arm and placing all three point estimates well inside the \(95\%\)
confidence intervals of the \(200\)-shopper human subsample:
\texttt{Nutrition\ Info} (\(\hat{\beta} = +0.153\)
vs.~\(\beta^* = +0.184\); base \(+0.137\)),
\texttt{Positive\ +\ Nutrition\ Info} (\(+0.146\) vs.~\(+0.179\); base
\(+0.132\)), and \texttt{Spectrum} (\(+0.078\) vs.~\(+0.108\); base
\(+0.005\)). Conversely, virtually all remaining OLS error is
concentrated in the two \textbf{asymmetric \texttt{High\ In} warning
arms} (\texttt{High\ In}: \(-0.048\) vs.~\(+0.054\);
\texttt{Positive\ +\ High\ In}: \(-0.001\) vs.~\(+0.119\)). In Grummon
et al. (2025), human shoppers viewed graphical package mockups in which
a black exclamation-mark warning shield visually signaled avoidance;
when serialized into text (\texttt{Front-of-package\ label:\ None} on
the healthier item versus
\texttt{Front-of-package\ label:\ High\ In:\ ...\ \textbar{}\ FDA.gov}
on the less healthy item), the asymmetric presence of an institutional
\texttt{FDA.gov} tag on only one option creates text-only salience
ambiguity without visual warning cues.

\textbf{Zero-shot generalization across seen versus unseen shopper
attributes.} To test whether respondent-level training memorizes the
demographic variables present in \texttt{US\ ISSP} and
\texttt{Twin-2K-500} or learns a generalizable mapping from
natural-language respondent descriptions to choice distributions, we
evaluate single-attribute conditioning across all \(10\) shopper
covariates (\(5\) seen in training versus \(5\) completely unseen
food-shopping and nutrition attributes; Panel B of
Figure~\ref{fig-grummon2025-coefs} and
\quartoapptblref{apptbl-grummon2025-personas}) as well as separate
\(5\)-attribute seen and unseen persona bundles. Off-the-shelf
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ (base)} degrades substantially on novel behavioral
covariates, averaging \texttt{1.5841} bits across the \(5\) unseen
single attributes versus \texttt{1.4932} bits across the \(5\) seen
attributes (and \texttt{1.5779} vs.~\texttt{1.4484} bits for the
\(5\)-attribute unseen vs.~seen bundles), performing worse than uniform
guessing (\texttt{1.5850} bits) when conditioned on
\texttt{Nutrition\ Facts\ label\ usage} (\texttt{1.7215} bits) or
\texttt{Share\ of\ groceries\ bought\ online} (\texttt{1.6270} bits).
Similarly, \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ (base)} spikes to \texttt{2.4561} bits on
\texttt{Share\ of\ groceries\ bought\ online}. Fine-tuning on general
survey microdata reduces choice cross-entropy on \textbf{all \(10\)
individual attributes} for both \texttt{Kev\ 4B\ FT} (\texttt{-17.9\%}
on seen; \texttt{-18.1\%} on unseen) and \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT}.
Crucially, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} achieves an even larger relative
cross-entropy reduction on the \(5\) \textbf{unseen} shopper attributes
(\texttt{1.5841} to \texttt{1.3956} bits, \(-11.9\%\); bundle
\texttt{1.5779} to \texttt{1.4100} bits, \(-10.6\%\))---including
\(-18.2\%\) on \texttt{Nutrition\ Facts\ label\ usage} (\texttt{1.7215}
to \texttt{1.4079} bits), \(-13.5\%\) on
\texttt{Share\ of\ groceries\ bought\ online} (\texttt{1.6270} to
\texttt{1.4075} bits), and \(-11.6\%\) on
\texttt{Nutrition\ literacy\ score} (\texttt{1.5481} to \texttt{1.3682}
bits)---than on the \(5\) \textbf{seen} demographic attributes
(\texttt{1.4932} to \texttt{1.3771} bits, \(-7.8\%\)), compressing the
seen-versus-unseen calibration gap nearly fivefold (\texttt{0.0909} to
\texttt{0.0185} bits).

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec-discussion}

\textbf{Resolving silicon-sampling bias upstream through architecture
and microdata alignment.} Our findings demonstrate that the severe
econometric biases documented in generative LLM silicon sampling (Goli
and Singh 2024, Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026) are neither an
inevitable property of foundation models nor remediable by scaling
test-time reasoning or fine-tuning autoregressive token generators on
small task demonstrations. Instead, accurate zero-shot preference and
treatment-effect recovery requires aligning both the model readout and
the training objective with human behavioral uncertainty.
Architecturally, single-pass decision models score valid options
directly in representation space, eliminating free-text parsing errors,
Monte Carlo sampling variance, and safety-filter refusals---thereby
retaining vaccine opt-out respondents that commercial generative LLMs
drop or distort---while outputting continuous within-respondent
probabilities that avoid the nearly eightfold MSE penalty of choice
discretization in Multinomial Logit estimation. Statistically,
optimizing cross-entropy across 1.88 million individual survey responses
(\texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500}) minimizes conditional
Kullback-Leibler divergence
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}) \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_q \mid \mathbf{s}; \theta))\)
at unit temperature (\(T = 1.00\)), amortizing calibration across
combinatorial multi-attribute personas and compressing the calibration
gap between seen demographic attributes and unseen behavioral covariates
nearly fivefold on \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT}.

\textbf{Implications for zero-shot pre-testing (\(m = 0\)) and future
integration with post-hoc calibration (\(m > 0\)).} For empirical
marketing researchers, distributional decision models expand the
workflow of AI-assisted research prior to primary data collection
(\(m = 0\)), such as screening candidate product attributes before
fielding a conjoint survey, pre-testing policy labels across demographic
subpopulations, or auditing experimental instruments at minimal marginal
compute (a single prefill forward pass per packed respondent profile).
At the same time, zero-shot silicon sampling complements rather than
replaces human experimentation for high-stakes managerial or regulatory
decisions, as even \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} exhibits larger estimation error
than a 200-participant human sample (\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.0239\) versus
\(0.0065\) in conjoint MNL; \(0.0056\) versus \(0.0016\) in trial OLS).
When target-domain human benchmarks are collected (\(m > 0\)), our
results motivate concrete directions for future research at the
intersection of upstream decision-model training and downstream
statistical calibration (Leng et al. 2026, Wang et al. 2026). In
AI-augmented estimation (\texttt{AAE}; Wang et al. (2026)), asymptotic
efficiency gains depend on the conditional informativeness of the
auxiliary AI output for human choices given product features
\(\mathbf{x}\) (their Proposition 2), rather than standalone marginal
calibration alone, while finite-sample error depends on learning the
first-stage mapping from \(m\) primary observations (their Theorem 2).
Investigating whether passing refusal-free, persona-conditioned
probability vectors \(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}, \mathbf{s}_i)\) from
\texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} into \texttt{AAE} injects respondent-level
variation beyond \(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}\) and simplifies first-stage link
estimation at small \(m\) is a promising next step. Similarly, in
\texttt{HuMCal} (Leng et al. 2026), adding \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} to a
candidate checkpoint pool enlarges the simplex and weakly lowers
in-sample calibration loss while escaping shared convex-hull inflation
among off-the-shelf models, whereas replacing checkpoints guarantees
non-increasing loss only under uniform dominance across all calibration
items and mixtures (their Proposition A-7)---a condition violated when
specific items attenuate or shift sign. Empirically characterizing the
out-of-sample bias-variance trade-offs of combining respondent-aligned
decision checkpoints with \texttt{HuMCal} and \texttt{AAE} remains an
important open question.

\textbf{Boundary conditions and methodological extensions.} Our
empirical evaluations also surface three clear boundary conditions that
guide future model development. First, our nutrition labeling RCT
analysis isolates a fundamental modality boundary of text-only silicon
sampling: while \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT} accurately recovers causal
treatment effects across all three symmetrical interpretive label arms
(\texttt{Nutrition\ Info}, \texttt{Positive\ +\ Nutrition\ Info}, and
\texttt{Spectrum}), it fails on the two asymmetric \texttt{High\ In}
warning arms where graphical black exclamation-mark shields were
serialized into asymmetric \texttt{FDA.gov} text strings. Extending
respondent-level distributional training to multimodal vision-language
decision heads that score visual package mockups, advertisements, and
shelf displays directly is therefore an immediate priority. Second,
because our training corpus (\texttt{US\ ISSP} and \texttt{Twin-2K-500})
centers on US social, political, and general behavioral attitudes rather
than commercial or clinical product trade-offs, \texttt{Kev\ 9B\ FT}
conservatively attenuates unfamiliar clinical risk-horizon attributes
(\texttt{Duration} and rare \texttt{Major\ side\ effects}) toward zero,
and intermediate scales (\texttt{Kev\ 4B\ FT}) can compress between-arm
differences despite lowering individual choice cross-entropy.
Incorporating historical commercial conjoint archives, scanner panels,
and cross-national survey modules beyond the United States would broaden
domain and cultural transfer. Third, although grouped shared-prefix
packing encodes each respondent's full answer set jointly during
training, zero-shot inference conditions only on observed covariates
\(\mathbf{s}_i\), treating repeated choices per respondent as
conditionally independent. Conditioning sequentially on prior observed
choices---or coupling decision heads with hierarchical Bayes and
mixed-logit random-coefficients models---could capture persistent
unobserved taste heterogeneity in panel settings.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Accurate silicon sampling for econometric inference
requires predicting calibrated conditional response distributions rather
than generating deterministic text completions. By pairing single-pass
decision heads with respondent-level cross-entropy training across 1.88
million public survey observations, distributional silicon sampling
eliminates sampling variance and safety refusals, generalizes to novel
respondent covariates, and substantially improves zero-shot parameter
recovery in conjoint MNL and experimental OLS regressions. Open-weight,
respondent-aligned decision models thus offer a practical and extensible
foundation for behavioral simulation in marketing science.
