\title{Distributional silicon sampling: training decision models to
predict human response distributions}
\author{Davood Wadi \and Yu Ma}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Researchers increasingly use foundation models to simulate human survey
responses across consumer segments, a practice known as silicon
sampling. Standard silicon sampling relies on generative large language
models (LLMs) that produce text one token at a time, requiring hundreds
of repeated draws to estimate a response distribution. Decision models
offer a direct alternative for silicon sampling because they take a
respondent persona and a structured Likert or multiple-choice question
and predict the full probability distribution over answer options in a
single forward pass. However, both generative LLMs and off-the-shelf
decision models are trained to maximize accuracy on objective tasks with
a single correct answer, such as coding and mathematics, which makes
them ill-suited for predicting the dispersed response distributions of
heterogeneous human populations. In this paper, we fine-tune three
open-weight decision models (Kev 0.8B, Kev 4B, and Kev 9B) to minimize
the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence against empirical human response
distributions from 10 waves of the US International Social Survey
Programme (\(14,356\) respondents and \(767,484\) responses across 616
questions). We show that training on this single survey dataset
substantially improves distributional predictions on unseen data across
three progressively challenging settings, namely unseen questions from
the same survey program, unseen questions and demographic personas from
the US General Social Survey (SubPOP), and an unseen marketing domain
from a randomized clinical trial on front-of-package nutrition labels
(\(5,636\) US shoppers across 6 labeling conditions and 21 choice and
Likert items). Notably, a small fine-tuned decision model (Kev 0.8B FT)
outperforms larger off-the-shelf baselines on unseen public opinion
questions, and fine-tuning improves distributional accuracy even on
nutrition-specific shopper personas never observed during training.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Simulating human attitudes, preferences, and survey responses with
foundation models, a paradigm known as silicon sampling, has attracted
growing interest in marketing, public opinion, and behavioral research
\citep{argyle2023out, horton2023large, brand2023using, goli2024frontiers, sarstedt2024using, pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, durmus2024towards, zahradnickova2026ai}.
In typical marketing applications, demographic marginals are already
known from census records or panel quotas, and the researcher's goal is
to predict the distribution of answers to Likert and multiple-choice
questions conditional on a target consumer persona. Currently,
researchers rely on generative large language models (LLMs) to perform
silicon sampling. Because generative LLMs produce free-form text one
token at a time, estimating a subpopulation response distribution
requires prompting the model hundreds of times via Monte Carlo sampling
\citep{wadi2025monte, zahradnickova2026ai} or applying post-hoc sample
calibration and statistical data augmentation to combine biased LLM
outputs with human benchmark data
\citep{leng2026reducing, wang2026large}. This repeated text generation
is slow, expensive with commercial APIs, sensitive to decoding
temperature and prompt wording, and prone to mode collapse or refusals
\citep{pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, bhatia2025value, liu2025badworktimecrosscultural, zahradnickova2026ai}.

Decision models, recently introduced as System One architectures
\citep{palmer2026kev, deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev, geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple, li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision},
offer a direct alternative for silicon sampling. Instead of generating
text tokens, a decision model takes a context state (e.g., a respondent
persona) and a structured question, such as an ordinal Likert scale
(\texttt{score}) or a categorical multiple-choice item
(\texttt{choice}), and predicts a probability distribution over the
supplied answer options in a single prefill forward pass. Because a
decision model outputs a normalized probability distribution over the
valid answer options directly, it eliminates the need for Monte Carlo
text sampling.

Despite this architectural advantage, both generative LLMs and
off-the-shelf decision models share a fundamental training mismatch when
applied to silicon sampling. Both model families are trained to maximize
accuracy on objective tasks that have a single ground-truth answer, such
as mathematics, code verification, factual question answering, and
deterministic routing
\citep{palmer2026kev, geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple, gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale}.
Specifically, standard training pairs each prompt with a single integer
class label and optimizes cross-entropy loss to push all probability
mass onto that one winning option. This objective is poorly suited for
tasks that require predicting a human response distribution. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-gender-mixed} across representative five-point Likert
questions from our US survey data, human respondents who share a
demographic attribute such as sex never agree unanimously (e.g., on how
partners share household work, \texttt{2022:v45}, both male respondents
with \(n = 274\) and female respondents with \(n = 236\) spread their
answers across all five rating levels). A model trained to concentrate
probability mass on a single correct answer treats genuine within-group
behavioral variation as classification error and cannot recover the true
response distribution of a consumer segment.

}

\caption{\label{fig-gender-mixed}Empirical US human response

To address this training mismatch, we fine-tune decision models directly
on empirical human response distributions and test whether training on a
single survey dataset improves silicon sampling on unseen data. We train
three open-weight pointer-head decision models of increasing size,
namely Kev 0.8B, Kev 4B, and Kev 9B \citep{palmer2026kev}, which are
built on Qwen 3.5 backbones \citep{qwen3.5}, to minimize the
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between predicted option distributions
and empirical human response shares across 10 waves of the US
International Social Survey Programme (ISSP 2015 to 2024; \(14,356\)
respondents and \(767,484\) responses across 616 general social attitude
questions). We then evaluate off-the-shelf and fine-tuned decision
models across three progressively challenging out-of-sample settings
without any post-hoc temperature tuning. First, we evaluate
generalization to unseen questions using 72 held-out US ISSP questions
(\(3,199\) subpopulation-question distributions). Second, we evaluate
generalization to unseen questions and demographic personas on the
external SubPOP benchmark \citep{suh-etal-2025-language}, which
comprises 133 US General Social Survey questions across 22
single-attribute subpopulations (\(2,926\) target distributions). Third,
to evaluate generalization to an entirely new marketing domain and
unseen consumer personas, we test our models on data from a randomized
clinical trial of front-of-package nutrition labels and simulated food
and beverage purchases \citep[\(5,636\) US grocery shoppers randomized
across 6 labeling conditions and 21 choice and Likert
questions;][]{grummon2025nutrition}. Across all three benchmarks and all
three model scales, training decision models on a single social survey
dataset consistently improves distributional predictions on new
questions, new personas, and a new marketing domain.

\section{Related work}\label{sec-related}

\subsection{Silicon sampling with generative
LLMs}\label{silicon-sampling-with-generative-llms}

Early work in silicon sampling prompted generative LLMs with demographic
backstories to simulate human responses in political, economic, and
marketing surveys
\citep{argyle2023out, horton2023large, brand2023using, goli2024frontiers, sarstedt2024using, pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, durmus2024towards}.
Although generative LLMs often capture the direction of average
preferences, prior studies document systematic discrepancies between
LLM-generated samples and human populations, including mode collapse
onto a single option, flattened within-group variance, demographic
stereotyping, and sensitivity to prompt formatting
\citep{pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, shu-etal-2024-dont, koo2024benchmarking, kabir-etal-2025-break, deas-mckeown-2025-artificial, jung-etal-2026-psychometric}.
Even when generative LLMs are provided with census reference
distributions in context or conditioned on real respondent profiles,
repeated Monte Carlo text draws still fail to match empirical response
distributions on Likert scales
\citep{wadi2025monte, zahradnickova2026ai}.

To mitigate these biases in marketing applications, recent studies have
proposed post-hoc statistical corrections that combine LLM outputs with
human benchmark data. In conjoint analysis, \citet{wang2026large}
develop a statistical data-augmentation estimator that blends
LLM-generated choices with human survey data to reduce estimation error,
showing that naive substitution of human data with raw LLM responses
introduces substantial bias. Similarly, \citet{leng2026reducing} propose
Human Mimicry Calibration (HuMCal), a post-hoc method that learns
optimal weights to combine LLM outputs across prompts, personas, or
checkpoints using aggregate human calibration benchmarks. A
complementary line of work in natural language processing directly
fine-tunes causal language models on aggregated survey distributions to
improve subpopulation alignment
\citep{suh-etal-2025-language, huang-etal-2026-distribution, ku2026siliconsamplingcrosssurveytransfer}.
Our work complements these approaches by shifting from autoregressive
text generation to decision models that directly output probability
distributions over survey options, and by demonstrating that
distributional fine-tuning on one general survey transfers zero-shot to
unseen personas and an unseen consumer nutrition experiment without
requiring target-domain calibration data.

\subsection{Decision models and direct probability
readouts}\label{decision-models-and-direct-probability-readouts}

To avoid the cost and sampling variance of free-text generation, several
studies extract next-token probabilities over option labels from causal
LLMs
\citep{kadavath2022language, kuribayashi-etal-2024-psychometric, bentegeac2025token, wadi2026every}.
Decision models formalize and extend this approach into a dedicated
single-pass architecture
\citep{palmer2026kev, deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev, geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple, li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision, asseri2026calibratedwhompersonalanguage, li2026kitescalingjevpopulation}.
Built on pretrained language model backbones \citep{qwen3.5}, decision
models compile a context state and typed questions (\texttt{score} for
ordered rating scales and \texttt{choice} for categorical options) into
a structured prompt and compute option probabilities in one prefill
pass. Open decision models such as Kev \citep{palmer2026kev} attach a
lightweight pointer head that scores the final-layer hidden state at
each option's closing token against the hidden state at the question's
closing token. Unlike vocabulary-letter readouts that map options to
arbitrary token symbols (e.g., ``A'', ``B'', ``C'') and remain
susceptible to token-frequency priors, a pointer head scores each option
directly from its contextualized text representation and restricts
probability mass strictly to the supplied options. However, because
existing decision models are trained on single-label objective
benchmarks, they exhibit severe scale compression on ordinal tasks
\citep{gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale}, motivating our
distributional training framework.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec-methodology}

\subsection{Predicting subpopulation response
distributions}\label{predicting-subpopulation-response-distributions}

Consider a survey dataset of human respondents indexed by
\(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Each respondent is characterized by
demographic and background attributes \(\mathbf{a}_i\) (e.g., sex, age,
education, income, and political party) and answers to survey questions
\(q \in \mathcal{Q}\). Each question \(q\) presents \(K_q\) discrete
answer options \(\mathcal{Y}_q = \{y_1, \dots, y_{K_q}\}\). For ordinal
Likert (\texttt{score}) items, the options are ordered from lowest to
highest as \(y_1 \prec y_2 \prec \dots \prec y_{K_q}\).

A persona \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) specifies a subset of attribute-value
pairs (e.g., ``Country: United States; Political party: Republican'').
Let \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)\) denote the set of human
respondents matching persona \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) who answered
question \(q\), with sample size
\(n_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q} = |\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)|\).
The empirical human response distribution over the \(K_q\) options is:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-human-pmf}{
P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) = \frac{1}{n_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)} \mathbb{I}(y_{i, q} = y_k), \quad k \in \{1, \dots, K_q\}.
}\end{equation}

In a pointer-head decision model \citep{palmer2026kev}, the persona
\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), question text \(q\), and option texts
\(\mathcal{Y}_q\) are formatted into a single prompt and processed in
one causal forward pass. Let
\(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) denote
the final-layer hidden state at the closing token of the question, and
let \(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{opt}, k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\)
denote the final-layer hidden state at the closing token of option
\(k\). Using learned linear projections
\(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}} \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}\),
the pointer head computes a score \(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k}\) for
each option \(k\):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-pointer-head}{
z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k} = \frac{(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{q}})^\top (\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{opt}, k})}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}.
}\end{equation} Applying a softmax function over the \(K_q\) option
scores yields the predicted response distribution in a single pass:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-model-pmf}{
P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) = \frac{\exp(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k} / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K_q} \exp(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, j} / T)},
}\end{equation} where \(T\) is the readout temperature (\(T = 1\) for
all fine-tuned models, with no post-hoc temperature fitting).

\subsection{Distributional training and evaluation
metrics}\label{distributional-training-and-evaluation-metrics}

To train a decision model to approximate human subpopulation
distributions rather than single-label classifications, we minimize the
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the empirical human
distribution \(P_{\mathrm{human}}\) and the model's predicted
distribution \(P_{\mathrm{model}}\) (measured in bits):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-kl}{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}} \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_q} P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_k) \log_2 \frac{P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_k)}{P_{\mathrm{model}}(y_k)}.
}\end{equation} During training, we optimize model parameters \(\theta\)
via soft cross-entropy loss against the empirical subpopulation shares:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-soft-ce}{
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_q} P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) \log P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k; \theta),
}\end{equation} which is equivalent to minimizing
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}} \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}})\) up to
a positive scaling constant and the fixed entropy of the human target
distribution. Because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) subtracts the entropy of
\(P_{\mathrm{human}}\) from the cross-entropy, questions on which human
respondents naturally disagree achieve \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0\) when the
model matches that spread.

We use \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) as our primary evaluation metric across all
choice and Likert questions. Because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) treats
categories as unordered, we also report the normalized 1-Wasserstein
distance (\(W_1\), or Earth Mover's distance) on ordinal Likert
(\texttt{score}) items
\citep{villani2008optimal, batterton2017likert, wadi2026every}:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-wasserstein}{
W_1(P_{\mathrm{human}}, P_{\mathrm{model}}) = \frac{1}{K_q - 1} \sum_{k=1}^{K_q - 1} \left| \sum_{j=1}^{k} P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_j) - \sum_{j=1}^{k} P_{\mathrm{model}}(y_j) \right| \in [0, 1],
}\end{equation} which measures the minimum fraction of the rating scale
across which probability mass must be moved to transform
\(P_{\mathrm{model}}\) into \(P_{\mathrm{human}}\).

We build our training dataset from 10 waves of US respondent-level
microdata from the International Social Survey Programme (ISSP 2015 to
2024, covering work orientations, role of government, social networks,
religion, social inequality, environment, health care, family and gender
roles, national identity, and digital societies). As summarized in
Table~\ref{tbl-datasets}, the dataset contains \(14,356\) unique US
respondents and \(767,484\) individual responses across 616 questions
(474 ordinal \texttt{score} items and 142 categorical \texttt{choice}
items), along with 15 harmonized respondent attributes, namely sex, age,
education, religion, religious service attendance, political ideology,
political party, past voting turnout, urban or rural residence, marital
status, employment status, union membership, subjective social status,
census region, ancestry, and household income.

To evaluate generalization to unseen questions without item leakage, we
partition the 616 US questions into 494 training questions (\(80.2\%\)),
50 validation questions (\(8.1\%\)), and 72 held-out test questions
(\(11.7\%\)). All cross-wave recurring questions are placed strictly in
the training split so that every validation and test question is unseen
during training. From the respondent-level training data, we construct
empirical subpopulation distributions by conditioning on single
demographic attributes (\(k = 1\), \(21,252\) records with
\(n \ge 100\)) and pairs of attributes (\(k = 2\), \(137,331\) records
with \(n \ge 100\)), supplemented by respondent-level profiles.

\subsection{Out-of-sample evaluation
benchmarks}\label{out-of-sample-evaluation-benchmarks}

We evaluate our models across three benchmarks that test generalization
across unseen questions, unseen personas, and an unseen marketing
domain.

Our first benchmark evaluates unseen questions on the 72 held-out US
ISSP test items (61 Likert \texttt{score} items and 11 \texttt{choice}
items). Conditioning on each single demographic attribute with at least
\(n = 100\) respondents yields \(3,199\) unseen subpopulation-question
distributions (\(2,772\) \texttt{score} records and \(427\)
\texttt{choice} records).

Our second benchmark evaluates unseen questions and personas on the
SubPOP evaluation split \citep{suh-etal-2025-language}, derived from the
US General Social Survey (GSS). It contains 133 survey questions (64
Likert \texttt{score} items and 69 categorical \texttt{choice} items)
evaluated across 22 single-attribute US demographic groups spanning
region, education, income, political ideology, political party, race,
religion, and sex, totaling \(2,926\) subpopulation-question target
distributions. None of the GSS questions or SubPOP's demographic
category definitions were used during training.

Our third benchmark evaluates an unseen marketing domain and unseen
consumer personas, testing whether a decision model trained only on
general social surveys can generalize to an applied consumer behavior
experiment. We use respondent-level data from
\citet{grummon2025nutrition}, a randomized clinical trial of
\(N = 5,636\) US primary grocery shoppers randomized to 1 of 6
front-of-package (FOP) nutrition labeling systems, namely Positive
labels, Nutrition Info labels, Positive plus Nutrition Info labels, High
In warning labels, Positive plus High In labels, and Spectrum (1-to-5
star) labels. Shoppers completed 21 survey items comprising 6
three-option product healthfulness choice tasks (identifying the
healthier item between paired beverages, breads, cereals, soups, snacks,
and frozen meals displaying arm-specific FOP labels), 2 binary choice
items on noticing and using the labels during simulated shopping, and 13
five-point Likert rating scales measuring perceived label helpfulness,
understandability, trust, policy support, negative emotions (worried,
scared, guilty, ashamed, and sad), health elaboration, and online store
realism. We evaluate models both at the trial-arm level
(\(6 \text{ arms} \times 21 \text{ items} = 126\) distributions) and at
the single-attribute subpopulation level across 10 shopper attributes
(\(5,060\) subpopulation-arm-question distributions across 42 persona
levels with \(n \ge 30\)). Crucially, while 4 of the 10 shopper
attributes overlap with demographic variables in ISSP (sex, age,
education, and household income), the remaining 6 attributes are unseen
during training, including race, Hispanic ethnicity, Supplemental
Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) participation, Nutrition Facts panel
usage frequency, share of groceries purchased online, and objective
nutrition literacy score.

\subsection{Models compared}\label{models-compared}

We compare three sizes of the open-weight Kev pointer-head decision
model family \citep{palmer2026kev}, namely Kev 0.8B (built on Qwen 3.5
0.8B), Kev 4B (built on Qwen 3.5 4B), and Kev 9B (built on Qwen 3.5 9B).
For each model size, we compare the released off-the-shelf baseline
model (\texttt{kev\ 0.8}, \texttt{kev\ 4B}, and \texttt{kev\ 9B},
evaluated at their released calibration temperatures) against our model
fine-tuned on the US ISSP training split (\texttt{kev\ 0.8\ FT},
\texttt{kev\ 4B\ FT}, and \texttt{kev\ 9B\ FT}, trained with rank-64
LoRA adapters and pointer heads and evaluated at \(T = 1.0\) without
post-hoc temperature tuning). As a naive reference point, we also report
a \texttt{Uniform} baseline that assigns equal probability \(1 / K_q\)
to each answer option.

outperforms both the 4B and 9B off-the-shelf baselines (\(0.3287\) and
\(0.3728\) bits) by a wide margin.

As shown in Table~\ref{tbl-grummon-personas} and
Figure~\ref{fig-grummon-personas}, off-the-shelf baselines degrade
noticeably when conditioned on unseen behavioral and nutrition
attributes such as Nutrition Facts panel usage (\texttt{NFPUSE}), online
grocery share (\texttt{ONLINEAMT}), and nutrition literacy
(\texttt{HEALTHLIT}). Fine-tuning on ISSP produces even larger
improvements on these unseen out-of-sample persona attributes than on
the in-training demographic attributes. Specifically, on the 6 unseen
shopper attributes, mean KL divergence drops from \(0.6805\) to
\(0.6203\) bits for Kev 0.8B (\(-8.8\%\)), from \(0.6642\) to \(0.5135\)
bits for Kev 4B (\(-22.7\%\)), and from \(0.5082\) to \(0.4351\) bits
for Kev 9B (\(-14.4\%\)), with particularly large gains on nutrition
literacy (\texttt{HEALTHLIT}, where KL drops from \(0.7745\) to
\(0.6231\) bits for Kev 0.8B and from \(0.5384\) to \(0.4192\) bits for
Kev 9B). These results demonstrate that training decision models on
human survey distributions recalibrates how the models express
population heterogeneity across Likert and choice options in general,
enabling zero-shot transfer to new consumer attributes and new marketing
domains.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec-discussion}

Silicon sampling in marketing and social science requires predicting how
a population of respondents distributes its answers across survey
options rather than generating a single text completion. While
generative LLMs require hundreds of Monte Carlo text draws or post-hoc
reweighting to approximate response distributions
\citep{wadi2025monte, leng2026reducing, wang2026large, zahradnickova2026ai},
decision models output a complete probability distribution over Likert
and choice options in a single forward pass \citep{palmer2026kev}.
However, because off-the-shelf decision models and generative LLMs are
trained for single-answer accuracy on objective benchmarks such as
coding and mathematics, they systematically underrepresent within-group
human diversity.

Our experiments show that fine-tuning decision models to minimize KL
divergence on empirical human response distributions from a single
social survey dataset (US ISSP) substantially improves distributional
silicon sampling on unseen data. Across Kev 0.8B, Kev 4B, and Kev 9B,
distributional fine-tuning reduces prediction error on held-out ISSP
questions, generalizes zero-shot to unseen questions and demographic
personas in the US General Social Survey (SubPOP), and transfers to an
applied marketing randomized clinical trial on front-of-package
nutrition labels \citep{grummon2025nutrition}, including consumer
persona attributes never observed during training. Moreover, our
findings show that a compact fine-tuned decision model (Kev 0.8B FT) can
outperform off-the-shelf models an order of magnitude larger on public
opinion benchmarks, offering marketing researchers a fast,
deterministic, and accessible tool for distributional silicon sampling.
