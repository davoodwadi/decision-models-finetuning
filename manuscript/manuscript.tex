\title{Can decision models sample human behavior? Evaluating
distributional alignment on human survey populations}
\author{Davood Wadi \and Yu Ma}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Simulating human survey responses with large language models (LLMs), a
paradigm often termed silicon sampling, typically relies on generative
text sampling or poorly calibrated token logits, resulting in mode
collapse, prompt brittleness, and distorted population variance.
Decision models have emerged as single-pass architectures that map a
context state and typed questions directly to calibrated probability
distributions over answer options in a single prefill forward pass. In
this paper, we investigate whether decision models, as well as causal
LLMs adapted via constrained option-letter readouts, can accurately
approximate empirical human response distributions across diverse
demographic subpopulations. Training on human survey data
(\(N = 458,753\)) and evaluating out-of-distribution on US
subpopulations from the General Social Survey (SubPOP; 2,926
subpopulation-question targets), we benchmark off-the-shelf and
fine-tuned decision models against standard causal LLMs. We show where
decision models succeed in capturing human marginal distributions and
inter-group contrasts, and where both architectures systematically fail
to reflect human behavioral heterogeneity.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Simulating human attitudes, preferences, and survey responses with
foundation models, a paradigm often termed silicon sampling or in silico
surveying, has attracted widespread interest across computational social
science, economics, and behavioral research
\citep{argyle2023out, horton2023large, brand2023using, sarstedt2024using, pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, durmus2024towards}.
However, using standard autoregressive large language models (LLMs) as
proxies for human populations has three fundamental limitations. First,
autoregressive text generation requires repeated Monte Carlo draws to
estimate a subpopulation's response distribution \citep{wadi2025monte},
introducing sampling noise, sensitivity to decoding temperature, and
fence-sitting refusals
\citep{bhatia2025value, liu2025badworktimecrosscultural}. Second,
next-token probabilities in instruction-tuned LLMs are often
miscalibrated, typically collapsing onto a single mode and failing to
reflect true inter-group variance across demographic attributes such as
sex, political ideology, or religion
\citep{pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, deas-mckeown-2025-artificial}. Third,
standard natural language processing metrics such as argmax accuracy,
Shannon entropy \citep{farquhar2024detecting}, or Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence alone treat survey options as unordered categories. On
ordinal Likert scales \citep{batterton2017likert}, order-invariant
metrics such as Shannon entropy cannot distinguish between a population
polarized at the extremes (e.g., strongly disagree versus strongly
agree) and one concentrated around adjacent moderate options (e.g.,
neutral versus agree) \citep{wadi2026every}.

Recently, a new class of decision model architectures, often referred to
as System One models in reference to dual-process theory
\citep{kahneman2011thinking, evans2013dual}, including TypeSafe's Jev,
Intern-Decision, and Kev
\citep{deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev, li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision, geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple},
has been introduced to replace free-form text generation with
structured, single-pass probabilistic decision-making. Given a context
state such as a respondent persona and a set of typed questions
comprising ordinal ratings (\texttt{score}), categorical multiple-choice
items (\texttt{choice}), and binary yes-or-no items (\texttt{noul}), a
decision model executes a single prefill-only forward pass and outputs a
probability mass function (PMF) over the supplied answer options via a
dedicated pointer head or a restricted option-token readout.

Because decision models are explicitly architected to output calibrated
distributions over discrete options without text generation, a natural
question arises as to whether they can sample human behavior.
Specifically, we examine how well off-the-shelf and domain-adapted
decision models, compared to standard causal LLMs
\citep{qwen3.5, suh-etal-2025-language}, approximate empirical human
response distributions and demographic contrasts.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{contributions}

This study makes three primary contributions to the literature on
silicon sampling and decision models. First, we provide a comprehensive
empirical benchmark of pointer-head decision models (Kev-0.8B, Kev-4B,
and Kev-9B) and constrained option-letter causal LLMs (Qwen3.5
\citep{qwen3.5}) across zero-shot, low-rank adaptation (LoRA),
partial-unfreezing, and full fine-tuning regimes, training on 10 waves
of respondent-level ISSP data (\(458,753\) individuals across 310
country-years) and testing out-of-distribution on US demographic
subpopulations from SubPOP \citep{suh-etal-2025-language}. Second, we
formulate an evaluation methodology tailored to categorical and ordinal
survey distributions that combines information-theoretic divergences
with the 1-Wasserstein transport distance \citep{villani2008optimal},
supplemented by an ordinal consensus metric
\citep{tastle2007consensus, wadi2026every} to diagnose scale compression
and polarization on Likert items. Third, we analyze inter-group
demographic contrasts to characterize when models collapse to marginal
national averages by ignoring persona conditioning, and demonstrate how
dynamic respondent-level attribute sampling during fine-tuning recovers
genuine human demographic divergences.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec-related}

\subsection{Simulating human populations with
LLMs}\label{simulating-human-populations-with-llms}

Early work in silicon sampling conditioned LLMs on demographic
backstories to approximate human economic, consumer, and public opinion
surveys
\citep{argyle2023out, horton2023large, brand2023using, sarstedt2024using, goli2024frontiers, pmlr-v202-santurkar23a, durmus2024towards}.
Subsequent studies explored fine-tuning and distributional alignment on
human survey distributions to improve subpopulation fidelity
\citep{suh-etal-2025-language, huang-etal-2026-distribution, ku2026siliconsamplingcrosssurveytransfer},
alongside broader investigations of cognitive and behavioral biases in
LLMs compared to human decision-makers
\citep{binz2023using, hagendorff2023human, macmillan2024ir, cheung2025large, echterhoff2024cognitive, mina2025cognitive}.
Despite this progress, the literature documents persistent failure
modes, including demographic stereotyping, flattening of within-group
variance, high sensitivity to prompt formatting and option order
\citep{shu-etal-2024-dont, kabir-etal-2025-break, koo2024benchmarking},
low ecological validity of generative outputs
\citep{jung-etal-2026-psychometric, deas-mckeown-2025-artificial}, and
poor alignment with non-WEIRD populations
\citep{henrich2010weirdest, zhao-etal-2024-worldvaluesbench}.

\subsection{From generative sampling to exact token probabilities and
decision
models}\label{from-generative-sampling-to-exact-token-probabilities-and-decision-models}

Recognizing the instability of free-text generation, recent work
extracts exact next-token probabilities over answer options rather than
sampling generated text
\citep{kuribayashi-etal-2024-psychometric, bentegeac2025token, kadavath2022language, wadi2026every}.
Extending this paradigm, decision models adapt pretrained causal LM
backbones \citep{qwen3.5} to ingest a context state and typed questions
(\texttt{score}, \texttt{choice}, and \texttt{noul}) and produce
calibrated probabilities in a single prefill pass
\citep{deusser2026evaluatingbenchmarkingmodeljev, asseri2026calibratedwhompersonalanguage, li2026kitescalingjevpopulation}.
Existing decision architectures rely on two primary readout mechanisms.
The first mechanism uses restricted next-token logits, mapping options
to single-token symbols (e.g., ``A'', ``B'', \(\dots\)) and taking a
softmax over allowed option logits at the answer position, as in
Intern-Decision, JevK5, and option-letter LLM readouts
\citep{li2026llmasjevllmsjevstyledecision, geuter2026releasinginnerjevsimple}.
The second mechanism uses a pointer head, scoring the hidden state at
each option's closing token against the question's final token via
learned linear projections as in Kev, which decouples option scoring
from vocabulary token priors. At the same time, recent evidence
indicates that direct-decision models can systematically underuse
ordinal rating scales through ordinal scale compression
\citep{gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale}, motivating rigorous
ordinal distributional evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluating ordinal probability
distributions}\label{evaluating-ordinal-probability-distributions}

Standard point estimates (e.g., expected values or top-1 accuracy) and
categorical uncertainty metrics (e.g., Shannon entropy
\citep{farquhar2024detecting}) fail on Likert scales because they ignore
the ordering and distance between response categories
\citep{batterton2017likert, joshi2015likert, wadi2026every}. To capture
the ordinal geometry of survey scales when comparing predicted
distributions against empirical human shares, we combine optimal
transport via the 1-Wasserstein distance \citep{villani2008optimal} on
discrete ordered supports with ordinal dispersion measures based on
consensus and dissension \citep{tastle2007consensus}.

\section{Problem formulation and architectures}\label{sec-problem}

\subsection{Task definition: subpopulation distributional
prediction}\label{task-definition-subpopulation-distributional-prediction}

Let a human survey dataset consist of respondents indexed by
\(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Each respondent has a vector of demographic
and background attributes \(\mathbf{a}_i\) (e.g., country, sex, age,
education, religion, and political party) and answers to survey
questions \(q \in \mathcal{Q}\). Each question \(q\) has \(K_q\)
discrete response options \(\mathcal{Y}_q = \{y_1, \dots, y_{K_q}\}\).
For ordinal Likert (\texttt{score}) items, \((\mathcal{Y}_q, \preceq)\)
is totally ordered from lowest to highest intensity.

A persona \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) specifies a subset of attribute-value
pairs (e.g., ``Country: United States; Political party: Republican'').
Let \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)\) denote the set of human
respondents matching persona \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) who provided a
valid answer to question \(q\), with group size
\(n_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q} = |\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)|\).
The empirical human response distribution (target PMF) is:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-human-pmf}{
P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) = \frac{1}{n_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, q)} \mathbb{I}(y_{i, q} = y_k), \quad k \in \{1, \dots, K_q\}.
}\end{equation}

Given the compiled state representation of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) and
the question specification \(q\), a model \(M\) outputs logits
\(\mathbf{z}_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_q}\) in a
single forward pass, defining the predicted PMF without temperature
rescaling (\(T = 1\)):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-model-pmf}{
P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}})_k = \frac{\exp(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K_q} \exp(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, j})}.
}\end{equation}

\subsection{Model architectures
compared}\label{model-architectures-compared}

We compare two distinct mechanisms for turning a causal language model
backbone into a distributional survey predictor.

\subsubsection{Pointer-head decision model
(Kev)}\label{pointer-head-decision-model-kev}

In the pointer-head architecture, the persona state and question options
are compiled into a structured prompt. Rather than reading from the
language modeling vocabulary head, a lightweight pointer head projects
the final-layer hidden state
\(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) at the
question's closing token and
\(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{opt}, k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) at
each option \(k\)'s closing token via learned matrices
\(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}} \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}\):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-pointer-head}{
z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k} = \frac{(\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{q}})^\top (\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{opt}, k})}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{head}}}}.
}\end{equation} Because options are scored directly through their
contextualized span representations rather than vocabulary tokens, the
pointer head natively handles arbitrary option counts, avoids
token-frequency biases, and assigns zero probability mass outside the
supplied options by construction.

\subsubsection{Constrained next-token letter
LLM}\label{constrained-next-token-letter-llm}

In the standard LLM readout, the persona and question options (labelled
``A.'', ``B.'', \(\dots\)) are formatted using the model's native chat
template with reasoning disabled, positioning the next predicted token
immediately at the assistant's response. Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be the full
vocabulary and
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{val}} = \{t_1, \dots, t_{K_q}\} \subset \mathcal{V}\)
be the single-token identifiers corresponding to option letters ``A''
through the \(K_q\)-th letter. Extracting the raw vocabulary logits at
the final prompt position yields
\(z_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, k} = \ell_{\mathrm{LM}}(t_k)\), and
applying Equation~\ref{eq-model-pmf} restricts and renormalizes the full
vocabulary distribution
\(P_{\mathrm{raw}}(t \mid x_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}})\) over
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{val}}\) \citep{cronbach1950further}. During
evaluation, we also track the pre-renormalization valid-letter mass
\(\sum_{t \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{val}}} P_{\mathrm{raw}}(t \mid x_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}})\)
to verify that the LLM concentrates probability mass on valid option
letters rather than conversational tokens.

\subsection{Fine-tuning on respondent-level human
data}\label{fine-tuning-on-respondent-level-human-data}

To test whether discrepancies between models and humans stem from
architectural limitations or a lack of behavioral calibration during
pretraining, we fine-tune both model families on respondent-level survey
data using soft cross-entropy loss against observed subpopulation
shares: \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-soft-ce}{
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K_q} P_{\mathrm{human}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k) \log P_{\mathrm{model}}(Y_{q, \boldsymbol{\lambda}} = y_k; \theta),
}\end{equation} which is equivalent to minimizing the Kullback-Leibler
divergence
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}} \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}})\) up to
a positive scaling constant and the fixed human target entropy
\(H(P_{\mathrm{human}})\).

\section{Methodology: evaluating subpopulation
distributions}\label{sec-methodology}

Evaluating how well \(P_{\mathrm{model}}\) approximates
\(P_{\mathrm{human}}\) requires more than top-1 accuracy or expected
value comparisons, because two distributions can share the exact same
mean while representing opposite sociological realities, such as
unimodal consensus versus bimodal polarization. For brevity, we write
\(P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_k)\) and \(P_{\mathrm{model}}(y_k)\) for a fixed
question \(q\) and persona \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). We evaluate model
predictions across within-group distributional fidelity, ordinal Likert
geometry and dispersion, and between-group demographic contrasts.

\subsection{Information-theoretic distributional
fidelity}\label{information-theoretic-distributional-fidelity}

For all question types (\texttt{choice}, \texttt{score}, and
\texttt{noul}), we measure information-theoretic divergence in bits
using the Kullback-Leibler divergence:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-kl}{
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}} \,\|\, P_{\mathrm{model}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_q} P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_k) \log_2 \frac{P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_k)}{P_{\mathrm{model}}(y_k)},
}\end{equation} along with the symmetric, bounded Jensen-Shannon
divergence
\(D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P_{\mathrm{human}}, P_{\mathrm{model}}) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{human}} \,\|\, M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathrm{model}} \,\|\, M) \in [0, 1]\),
where \(M = \frac{1}{2}(P_{\mathrm{human}} + P_{\mathrm{model}})\).
Because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) subtracts the target's own entropy from the
cross-entropy, questions on which human respondents naturally disagree
are not penalized when the model matches that spread.

\subsection{Ordinal transport distance and dispersion on Likert
scales}\label{ordinal-transport-distance-and-dispersion-on-likert-scales}

Because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{JS}}\) are
permutation-invariant, they penalize a shift from strongly agree to
agree identically to a shift from strongly agree to strongly disagree.
For ordinal Likert (\texttt{score}) items with ordered levels
\(y_1 \prec \dots \prec y_{K_q}\) indexed by \(\{1, \dots, K_q\}\), we
capture the scale geometry using the 1-Wasserstein distance (Earth
Mover's distance), computed as the \(L_1\) distance between cumulative
distribution functions (CDFs) \(F_{\mathrm{human}}\) and
\(F_{\mathrm{model}}\) normalized by the scale range \(K_q - 1\):
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-wasserstein}{
W_1(P_{\mathrm{human}}, P_{\mathrm{model}}) = \frac{1}{K_q - 1} \sum_{k=1}^{K_q - 1} \left| \sum_{j=1}^{k} P_{\mathrm{human}}(y_j) - \sum_{j=1}^{k} P_{\mathrm{model}}(y_j) \right| \in [0, 1].
}\end{equation} Here, \(W_1\) measures the minimum fraction of the scale
range across which probability mass must be transported to transform the
model's distribution into the empirical human distribution, with
\((K_q - 1) W_1\) giving the unnormalized transport error in Likert
levels.

To diagnose whether ordinal errors stem from scale compression
\citep{gao2026choicesfewerdecisionsordinalscale} or failure to capture
human polarization, we supplement \(W_1\) with the ordinal consensus
metric \citep{tastle2007consensus, wadi2026every}. For an ordinal item
with integer support \(\mathcal{Y}_q = \{1, \dots, K_q\}\), mean
\(\mu = \sum_{k=1}^{K_q} k \, P(y_k)\), and scale width
\(d_{\max} = K_q - 1\), ordinal consensus \(\mathrm{Cns}(P) \in [0, 1]\)
is defined as:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-consensus-univariate}{
\mathrm{Cns}(P) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{K_q} P(y_k) \log_2 \left( 1 - \frac{|k - \mu|}{d_{\max}} \right),
}\end{equation} with dissension given by
\(\mathrm{Dsn}(P) = 1 - \mathrm{Cns}(P)\). Consensus equals \(1\) when
all probability mass concentrates on a single rating level and equals
\(0\) when mass splits equally between the two extreme endpoints \(y_1\)
and \(y_{K_q}\). The difference
\(\Delta \mathrm{Cns} = \mathrm{Cns}(P_{\mathrm{model}}) - \mathrm{Cns}(P_{\mathrm{human}})\)
indicates whether a model is over-concentrated
(\(\Delta \mathrm{Cns} > 0\)) or over-dispersed
(\(\Delta \mathrm{Cns} < 0\)) relative to empirical human respondents.

\subsection{Inter-group demographic
contrasts}\label{inter-group-demographic-contrasts}

A central requirement of silicon sampling is capturing how attitudes
vary across demographic subpopulations rather than outputting the same
marginal distribution regardless of persona conditioning. For any
question \(q\) and any two subpopulations \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_a\) and
\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_b\) that differ on a demographic attribute (e.g.,
democrat versus republican, male versus female, or high versus low
income), we quantify the inter-group divergence within either the human
population or a model
\(P \in \{P_{\mathrm{human}}, P_{\mathrm{model}}\}\) via the normalized
1-Wasserstein contrast gap:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-contrast-gap}{
G_q(P; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b) = W_1\big(P(\cdot \mid \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a),\, P(\cdot \mid \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b)\big).
}\end{equation} When
\(G_q(P_{\mathrm{model}}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b) \approx 0\)
on questions where the empirical human gap
\(G_q(P_{\mathrm{human}}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b)\)
is large, the model exhibits persona blindness, collapsing to a single
marginal response profile across groups. We evaluate contrast recovery
both through the contrast gap error
\(\Delta G_q = G_q(P_{\mathrm{model}}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b) - G_q(P_{\mathrm{human}}; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b)\)
and through the directional agreement and correlation of signed mean
shifts
\(\Delta \mu_q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b) = \mu_q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_a) - \mu_q(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_b)\)
between model predictions and human subpopulations.

\subsubsection{Dynamic respondent-level sampling
(ISSP)}\label{dynamic-respondent-level-sampling-issp}

Rather than aggregating respondents into a fixed small set of
demographic cells, which averages away attributes such as religion,
political party, or income, our training sampler dynamically constructs
persona-question records on the fly from respondent-level ISSP data.
Specifically, for each training example, the sampler first draws a
(year, country, question) slot uniformly at random. Next, it draws an
attribute count \(k \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, A_{\text{avail}})\) from up
to 16 background attributes, namely sex, age, education, religion,
religious attendance, party vote, left-right party position, turnout,
household income, region, urban or rural residence, marital status, main
activity, union membership, subjective social status, and ancestry.
Finally, it samples an observed attribute combination with at least
\(n_{\min}\) matching respondents and computes their exact empirical
answer shares as the training target.

\subsubsection{Out-of-distribution test set (SubPOP /
GSS)}\label{out-of-distribution-test-set-subpop-gss}

We evaluate all models on the SubPOP evaluation split
\citep{suh-etal-2025-language}, comprising 133 US General Social Survey
questions across 22 single-attribute US subpopulations (2,926 total
target distributions, consisting of 64 ordinal Likert \texttt{score}
items and 69 categorical \texttt{choice} items) after excluding items
that overlap with ISSP training questions. No temperature scaling
(\(T=1\)) is applied at test time.

\subsection{Models and training
regimes}\label{models-and-training-regimes}

We evaluate three primary model families and training configurations
across identical data splits. First, we benchmark off-the-shelf
pointer-head decision models (Kev-0.8B, Kev-4B, and Kev-9B), which pair
pretrained Qwen3.5 backbones with released LoRA adapters and pointer
heads trained on general decision benchmarks. Second, we adapt the Kev
models on ISSP across several parameter-efficient and full-parameter
regimes, either continuing training from the released adapter, training
a fresh rank-\(R\) LoRA adapter (\(R \in \{16, 64, 128, 256\}\))
alongside the pointer head, unfreezing the top-\(L\) transformer decoder
layers (\(L \in \{2, 3, 6\}\)), or fine-tuning all backbone weights
together with the pointer head. Within these fine-tuned decision models,
we also conduct data construction ablations comparing all 310 ISSP
country-years against US-only respondents, varying persona attribute
count \(k\), and varying the minimum respondent threshold
\(n_{\min} \in \{1, 10, 100\}\). Third, to isolate the effect of the
pointer head against a standard vocabulary readout, we evaluate
Qwen3.5-0.8B both zero-shot and fine-tuned end-to-end through its
language modeling head over option letters ``A'' through ``L''.

\subsubsection{Key findings (RQ1)}\label{key-findings-rq1}

Off-the-shelf decision models are trained primarily for deterministic
classification and routing, causing them to produce overly sharp,
miscalibrated distributions when asked to represent a heterogeneous
human subpopulation. Fine-tuning with soft cross-entropy on empirical
human response shares substantially reduces both \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\)
across all question types and ordinal \(W_1\) transport error on Likert
scales. Comparing the pointer-head model and the constrained
option-letter LLM on identical Qwen3.5 backbones and ISSP training
recipes isolates the contribution of the pointer readout relative to
vocabulary-token scoring.

\subsection{Ordinal scale alignment and dispersion on Likert items
(RQ2)}\label{ordinal-scale-alignment-and-dispersion-on-likert-items-rq2}

Across the 64 Likert (\texttt{score}) questions in the SubPOP evaluation
split, we evaluate how well models capture the ordered geometry of
rating scales via \(W_1\) and diagnose their internal dispersion via
\(\Delta \mathrm{Cns}\). Off-the-shelf decision models exhibit large
transport errors alongside strongly positive consensus bias
(\(\Delta \mathrm{Cns} > 0\)), reflecting ordinal scale compression and
mode collapse onto a single rating option. While distributional
fine-tuning reduces \(W_1\) and brings model dispersion closer to human
consensus levels overall, we examine whether fine-tuned models
successfully replicate bimodal human polarization on contentious
sociopolitical questions or instead regress toward unimodal,
center-peaked distributions.

\subsection{Capturing inter-group demographic contrasts
(RQ3)}\label{capturing-inter-group-demographic-contrasts-rq3}

To test whether models capture true sociological heterogeneity rather
than memorizing a single marginal distribution per question, we evaluate
inter-group Wasserstein contrast gaps
\(G_q(P; \boldsymbol{\lambda}_a, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_b)\) and signed
mean shifts across contrasting subpopulations, such as democrat versus
republican, male versus female, atheist versus protestant, and high
versus low income, on items exhibiting the largest empirical human gaps.
Furthermore, we show why fixed coarse aggregation (e.g., grouping only
by country, sex, age, and education) induces persona blindness on
unconditioned attributes, and how respondent-level dynamic subset
sampling restores sensitivity to demographic conditioning.

\subsection{Ablation studies (RQ4)}\label{ablation-studies-rq4}

We conduct four ablation studies to isolate the drivers of
distributional alignment. First, we examine model scale and adaptation
depth across 0.8B, 4B, and 9B backbones, comparing LoRA rank against
top-layer unfreezing and full fine-tuning. Second, we vary persona
construction and minimum group size (\(n_{\min}\)), contrasting
single-respondent one-hot targets (\(n_{\min}=1\)) against aggregated
subpopulation shares (\(n_{\min} \in \{10, 100\}\)) and varying the
number of conditioning attributes \(k\). Third, we compare
cross-national training on all 310 ISSP country-years against
single-country training on US-only ISSP modules when testing on US GSS
subpopulations. Fourth, we evaluate the impact of prompt augmentation
(e.g., shuffling option orders and reversing Likert scales during
training) relative to canonical option ordering on test-time
calibration.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

\subsection{When do decision models make sense for sampling human
behavior?}\label{when-do-decision-models-make-sense-for-sampling-human-behavior}

Decision models offer fast, deterministic single-pass inference
(\(O(1)\) prefill per question), exact restriction to the supplied
option set via pointer heads, and a direct representation of
subpopulation marginals \(P(Y_q \mid \boldsymbol{\lambda})\) when
trained with distributional cross-entropy. However, because decision
models evaluate questions conditionally independently given the persona
state,
\(P(\mathbf{Y} \mid \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \prod_q P(Y_q \mid \boldsymbol{\lambda})\),
sampling from output PMFs reproduces subpopulation marginals but does
not automatically capture within-person cross-question covariance unless
the conditioning attributes fully explain the correlation across items.
In addition, predicting novel cultural or temporal shifts solely from
demographic labels remains bounded by out-of-domain attitude
generalization limits.

\subsection{Limitations and ethical
considerations}\label{limitations-and-ethical-considerations}

Using silicon samples carries the risk of replacing underrepresented
human voices in policy, marketing, or public health research if deployed
uncritically. Moreover, demographic and geographic coverage biases in
cross-national survey training corpora bound the populations for which
distributional predictions remain valid.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec-conclusion}

We investigated whether decision models and constrained next-token LLMs
can serve as calibrated samplers of human behavior. By pairing
respondent-level cross-national survey training on ISSP with a
distributional evaluation on SubPOP across information-theoretic
divergences, ordinal 1-Wasserstein distance and scale dispersion, and
inter-group demographic contrasts, we demonstrated both the
effectiveness of distributional fine-tuning and the structural limits of
current architectures in replicating human behavioral heterogeneity.
